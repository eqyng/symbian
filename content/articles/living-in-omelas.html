Living in Omelas

A reflection on moral responsibility in AI design

 06 Sep '26

The Ones who walk away from Omelas by Ursula K LeGuin is a short story about the city of Omelas, the most joyous city. Any abundance you can imagine lives there - wise and kind citizens, poignant music, the fairest weather imaginable.

But at a price.

A young child lives in the city's basement - alone, in squalor, and fear. If the child is brought above ground, or spoken to, the city will be destroyed.

And the citizens are aware. From a young age everyone has seen the child. Its story is passed down so they understand the city's beauty and their joy rests on its suffering.

The youths return home, angry and helpless. But, in time, they realise they can do no good anyway, and continue to enjoy the city's prosperity.

Still others leave Omelas in the quiet of the night, passing through the once boisterous streets, past the farmlands. And no one knows what happens to them.

Stories as Embodied Lessons

The description of Omelas is not so much provided by Le Guin, as it is co-created with us. The narrator, someone who knows of Omelas but is presumably not a citizen, asks us to envision this city of joy. Of comfort, ease, luxury, even drugs and orgies if that's what's needed to convince us that it exists: "Do you believe? Do you accept the festival, the city, the joy? No?".

There's an assumption that we can't conceive of happiness if it doesn't have a cost. It is only after they describe the child do they ask us, "Now do you believe them? Are they not more credible?"

The child's suffering is described in visceral detail. We understand that it wasn't born into this pain, it had a mother and the outside world. It was old enough to know true joy, and now knows true pain. Yet it's also young enough to try and rationalise its punishment as something it can change through behaviour. The difference, and impossible negotiation is cruel.

the child, who has not always lived in the tool room, and can remember sunlight and its mother's voice, sometimes speaks. "I will be good, " it says. "Please let me out. I will be good!" They never answer. The child used to scream for help at night, and cry a good deal, but now it only makes a kind of whining, "eh-haa, eh-haa," and it speaks less and less often.

However, writing a fable allows us to push boundaries to explore our moral stances.

Learning Lessons

The two pathways presented - stay in the city with the knowledge, or walk away. A third way of stay in the city to try and fix the child's suffering is absent. The binary choice makes this alternative structurally impossible, and the moral question so stark.

Institutions often use a similar framing to justify decisions like innovation as a blanket rule because "there's no other way". The plausibility structure is imbibed as rule and woven into the culture, much like the Omelas citizens' quiet despair. If there is specificity, there is space to contest it. Further, the only individuals who see it are the adolescents, those who have yet to take on the culture as 'normal'.

Like the adolescents we are at a crossroads with AI. Our narratives are still shifting, and we are still negotiating the invisible frames of meaning. What makes this definition urgent is whoever determines the baseline means there is a societal pressure to maintain this status quo which may not be in our best interests.

The unfortunate reality is that we are already living in Omelas. We are surrounded by gilded palaces of productivity and tools of innovation. But these dreams also sit beside unintended harms to vulnerable populations like children.

Despite Le Guin's story making the third way structurally impossible, governance is a lever that we can operate in spite of that. The aim is to make sure we have a clear line of sight in terms of decision traceability and reliable monitoring so AI features and products function as intended.

Reading this story made me feel reflective for a few reasons.

I realised a striking parallel between the story and my previous article of Hiding the Machine in the Man. I had a sinking realisation that I am living in the city of Omelas.

We laughed, we felt discomfort when we recognised the customer had made a connection to our AI voice agent. But that did not change our design decisions. There's no time, we rationalise to ourselves.

And it has remained a story I tell over dinner.

I'm still not sure what to make of this realisation. Only that I understand that a designer has an inescapable moral responsibility to help build a transparent infrastructure, and mitigate children sliding into basements without our knowledge.
