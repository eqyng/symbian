Book Notes - Show your work! (Kleon, 2014)

Kleon's 10 tips for (creative) authenticity

Last Tended: 26.07.21

Summary of the Summary:

*Kleon’s 10 tips for (creative) authenticity:* Do stuff. A lot of stuff. And let people in on your process while you’re doing that stuff. Share what you love whole-heartedly and honour those parts of you. Do this for long enough and your people will find you whether it’s for work or to create a community.

*Q’s Thoughts:*

I know the crushing feeling of having something to share, but not enough courage to share it, the works that remain only in the works, the hundreds of ideas that become thousands. But Kleon’s book has become one of my inspirations in seeing sharing differently - as a form of ‘truth-telling’, and self-expansion through others witnessing. This book isn’t just for the creative folks either, it’s for anyone really. Sharing is an act of human connection. (I know Ali Abdaal said that we also had no reason not to read the book because it took less than 30 minutes but I am not ((a god)) Ali Abdaal).

If I had to do a quote summary of the summary, I’d use the one that Kleon introduced his first chapter with:

That’s all any of us are: amateurs. We don’t live long enough to be anything else — Charlie Chaplin

It’s a little bittersweet, but he’s right. We’re not perfect, and we don’t have time. So “have the guts” (as Kleon says) to express yourself, explore, play. There are infinite pathways each of us could’ve chosen, but we don’t live long enough to live them all. Show your work, and allow the rest of us to come with you on your journeys.

*Summary Notes:*

Creativity in the digital age is *a new way of operating* because you need to be both good and findable. On the bright side you can be findable while getting good. Think about it as an alternative to self-promotion, or just a way to look out for your people.

1. You don’t have to be a genius

The “lone genius” is out, “community” is in - creativity is not a solo sport, you need people to share with, connect to, support from. Talk about what you care about, the people who care about the same things will hear you. This is how you *find a scenius, and how your scenius finds you. You can’t find your voice if you don't use it. I’ll repeat that for my sake: You can’t find your voice if you don’t use it. So, be an amateur with enthusiasm. Even if you don’t think you’re that great, you’re at least on the spectrum, and working towards great with something is whole lot easier than with nothing. If you need another way to stoke the fires of motivation, read obituaries*.

Amateurs are just regular people who get obsessed by something and spend a ton of time thinking out loud about it so that others can learn [from their failures and successes] — Austin Kleon

2. Think Process, Not Product

You have to make stuff…No one is going to give a damn about your résumé; they want to see what you have made with your own little fingers — David Carr

Humans are inherently interested in other humans. Whatever your work is, there’s an art to it. When you *take people behind the scenes and become a documentarian of what you do*, you’re allowing people to be part of the creative process and evolve alongside you. This way of relationship-building is unique to the digital age.

3. Share Something Small Everyday

You always have something to share wherever you are in your process. Try *send out a daily dispatch to gauge how you’re doing through your audience’s reactions. This is how you turn your flow into stock - these small updates will compound into durable content that people will use to search you up weeks, months, years later. But share work that passes the “So What?” Test. It’s a fine line but there’s a difference between sharing and oversharing i.e. do they pass gatekeepers like “Is this helpful?” “Is it entertaining?” “Is this something I’d be comfortable with my boss or my mother seeing?” And if you really want to share, plus have complete control over your work, consider registering a site where you can build a good (domain) name.* (actually, Kleon phrased it less of a thing to consider and more of a must do, no questions)

Whether people show up or they don’t, you’re out there, doing your thing, ready whenever they are — Austin Kleon

4. Open Up Your Cabinet of Curiosities

Our tastes and influences make up who we are and what we do - music, inspiration, art, movies. Open up your mental scrapbook, *don’t be a hoarder. And no guilty pleasures - there’s no such thing. The world needs diversity and breadth, and celebration of these colourful differences. But credit is always due* (e.g. you can find Austin Kleon here!). Without proper attribution you are not only robbing the original creator but also the people who want to make that thing a part of their own identity. Good rule of thumb: if you can’t find the credit, don’t share.

5. Tell Good Stories

You don’t exist in a vacuum, and neither does your work. Your *work doesn’t speak for itself, so you need to create a story and context for it. Structure is everything in good storytelling. It orients people in how to feel, interpret and value your work. Keep in mind that your work is often tied to you, so when you talk about yourself at parties*, use it as an opportunity to connect with someone.

6. Teach What You Know

As soon as you learn something, teach it, *share your trade secrets*. When you give away knowledge freely, you’re also receiving an education on how to improve.

7. Don’t Turn Into Human Spam

Following on from the last point, *shut up and listen because if you want to get something, you must give - whether it’s your time, attention, or curiosity. You want hearts, not eyeballs. Who you know depends on who you are and what you do. Plus, who you want to know is whoever passes the vampire test (applicable to other areas in life). It’s also how you identify your fellow knuckleballers, your real peers. And as much as you can, meet up in meatspace* not just online.

8. Learn to Take a Punch

Compulsive avoidance of embarrassment is a form of suicide — Colin Marshall

Putting your work out there also means accepting the positive feedback with the negative. So *let ‘em take their best shot, practice getting hit…a lot (apparently Mike Monteiro and his classmates were savages to each other). Then make even more work. Remember: don’t feed the trolls*. Ensure these comments are vetted to those who want to see you improve. And beware the troll that lives in your head.

9. Sell Out

Many artists have the notion that commerce corrupts creativity. But *even the Renaissance had to be funded. If a new opportunity comes along that allows you to expand and grow, take it - make more work for yourself. Or at least enough so you can be productively selfish in balancing work and time. And don’t be afraid to pass around the hat and ask for donations or charge a fair price. It helps you keep doing what you’re doing (and shows how much your work is actually valued). When you keep a mailing list, you can keep connected to the people who may be some of your biggest supporters, they’ve agreed to be spammed. But most of all - pay it forward*.

recognize that if you have had success, you have also had luck— and with luck comes obligation. You owe a debt, and not just to your gods. You owe a debt to the unlucky. — Michael Lewis

10. Stick Around

*Don’t quit your show prematurely. Chain-smoke your projects so you can keep the momentum going, and when you can’t, go away so you can come back - take a break, go on a sabbatical, go for a walk in nature. When you’ve learned the ins and outs of your work, don’t be afraid to begin again*. The lessons you learned in the old project will be with you for the next, you can’t get rid of them even if you tried.

You can’t count on success; you can only leave open the possibility for it, and be ready to jump on and take the ride when it comes for you. — Austin Kleon

Kleon’s closing comments:

  • Go online and post what you’re working on right now with the tag #showyourwork
  • Plan a “Show Your Work!” night with colleagues or friends. Use this book as a guide — share works-in-progress and your curiosities, tell stories, and teach one another

  • Give a copy of this book away to somebody who needs to read it
