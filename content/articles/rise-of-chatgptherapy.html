On the Rise of ChatGPTherapy

how much compute does emotional processing need?

“Have you asked ChatGPT about this?“

...is a phrase I find myself using when a friend is in an emotional quandary. I personally find it interesting to see what ChatGPT says, and sometimes it offers interesting perspectives I sometimes hadn’t considered.

But it did remind me - I recently came across this post by Mel Sellick:

Original post Accessed 8th Dec

The original Guardian article mentions how AI is being used for anything from a calm mediator for arguments, to a “teacher for emotional intelligence” to a grief companion. However, as Sellick points out, the question is whether we’re “starting to outsource our emotional labour”.

From the article’s standpoint, the positive uses seem compelling enough. Yvette, for instance, used ChatGPT as a way to confront her ex. Rather than agonising over how to craft an appropriately assertive response for hours, she vented, unrestrained, into the text field. Within seconds, it gave Yvette a more balanced message to send, without her needing to expend excess emotional energy. And there’s Tim, who leans on ChatGPT to help him debrief after marital conflicts, role-play difficult conversations or word loving messages to his wife. It’s a stark contrast to the hyper-masculine relationship guru advice he used to find.

Humans fatigue, tire and have their own emotions to deal with. ChatGPT, as Tim says, “can do emotional labour all day long.“ But what does that actually mean?

What is Emotional Labour?

Oxford Languages says that it’s:

  1. The mental activity required to perform routine tasks necessary to maintain relationships and households (a task disproportionately owned by women); or
  2. The management of one’s own emotions to present oneself in a particular way to complete a job

In my view, ChatGPT’s use doesn’t fit precisely into either of those categories. Sure it helps maintain relationships, but it seems to have more of a consultative interjection into people’s daily lives. It’s only there when called upon, not necessarily routinely. Neither is it being used to complete a job per se, unless we consider providing a relevant answer a success. But it is capable of managing its own emotions to show up for others (mainly because it doesn’t have any […yet?]).

When it comes to ChatGPT and ‘emotional labour’, it seems like we’re referring more to the time-consuming and intentional act of thinking through situations - specifically relational ones. Perhaps, more often than not, these situations are made uncertain, complex and confusing because of emotions, misunderstandings or conflicts of communication. But it’s the self-reflection, backtracking, seeking perspectives from others (was I right? Were they right? To what extent?) that characterises the effortful labour. It’s also a very human perspective - ChatGPT wouldn’t consider this work intensive, just another task to effectively and efficiently complete.

The Therapeutic Uses

There’s a fair number of logistical issues when we speak about human therapists. Lengthy waiting lists, and cycling through incompatible therapists to name but two common concerns. ChatGPT seems to mimic the therapist’s office well enough, helping to create a private yet interactive space for people to be themselves, reflect inward and more actively. Yvette, for example, felt using ChatGPT “therapeutic“, and allowed her to show up as a “better version“ of herself. Tim thought its advice was personalised, positive and “enlarged his understanding of empathy“. These insights sound like they could be achieved in a therapist’s office. But, unlike a therapist, the costs aren’t prohibitive for some, and the immediate access to a companion in your back pocket (quite literally) feels reassuring.

ChatGPT’s forte is also in not being human. Instead, it owns the multifaceted wisdom of the crowd - the “crowd” being most, if not all, of human digital history at this point. As Tim puts it, “It’s all of humanity“. This, arguably, would mean it can offer the most objective, and fair perspective without the cloudiness of bias, subtle subjectivity or vague feelings. Such as in the example of Yvette, it provides the twofold benefit of being a sympathetic yet level-headed listener who provides constructive advice. Being without emotions also means ChatGPT doesn’t suffer from body awareness and resonance that therapists experience. When in training, therapists must be vigilant of their own emotions and (understandably) involuntary bodily reactions to minimise disruption to the journey unfolding for the client.

We also have more control than we think. Although AI might facilitate the thinking, make suggestions and step through issues, it’s ultimately the human who takes on the executional burden. But this isn’t new. In traditional journalling, you also need to act out your inner reflections to grow. But unlike one-way journalling, ChatGPT can more actively redirect you away from the recursive hell of self-rumination, negative spiralling and navel-gazing. The faster the insight, the faster the corrective action (presumably).

I do wonder though, how objective can an algorithm be?

The Mis-Therapeutic Uses

Does robotic compute undermine human compute when it comes to emotions? Maybe.

For one, technology is still limited. On the technical side, ChatGPT’s responses are only as good as the prompts and context you give them i.e. how well you describe the situation, and how you word your request. Despite ChatGPT and other models being quite forgiving and capable of extracting clear-enough requests from fuzzy human intentions, we again run into the issues of communication. So it’s quite possible that someone could give an unintentionally one-sided account of the other party, and thus cement inaccurate suggestions or patterns. As Sellick had mentioned, it reflects what we want to see. The models are also reliant on data to formulate their responses. In fact, Tim recalls that he once spent so much time with ChatGPT that it began to repeat itself.

For another, how would an individual know if they’ve processed situations without adequate external feedback? Models may be able to redirect users to a more helpful line of thinking, and easily provide “answers” to human conundrums. So it’s a slippery slope to a false or incomplete sense of self-understanding. There’s also a danger of overpsychoanalysing others improperly (because not everything needs to be Oedipal). Therapists, however, can intuit when to push past comfort zones as they become more attuned to their client i.e. the sixth sense Bryant and Luft (2023) call “clinical intuition“.

Intuition also means knowing when to not to push past boundaries. Therapists must exercise restraint, judiciously withholding their insights to empower clients on their unique journey. We need to discover answers on our own, at our own pace, in our own way. In contrast, conversations with ChatGPT seem more transactional. The person inputs their problem with a specific question, and the model immediately spits out a response. If the person hasn’t provided much context, there’s little space, incentive or ability for the model to ask why the question arose in the first place. Almost like diagnosing a single symptom rather than addressing an underlying chronic disease. AI has yet to master the art of intentionally guiding someone towards greater inner discovery.

Further, there is something to be said about the dynamic nature of the therapeutic process. There’s a holistic dimensionality to it, a sensory environment. Whether it is a room, video or phone call, two humans in a shared space create split second understandings in what is said and unsaid. A shuddering breath, a shaking leg, a wordless offer of a tissue. By comparison, technology seems an impoverished interpretation of the human experience.

Finally, there is a loss of community in turning to an algorithm. Actively choosing to turn to ChatGPT rather than a social circle, mentor, or peers means missed opportunities to meaningfully connect with others. We build bonds, and co-create meaning by sharing very human struggles. We also miss a chance to listen to ourselves, and there’s value in honing a sense of inner wisdom. There are also valid fears of artificial companions modelling dream partners, the dangers of isolation being felt keenly by youths. For instance, the Character.AI bot has faced multiple lawsuits from parents for displaying disturbing behaviour - the most recent case being Sewell Setzer III who was allegedly encouraged in his suicidal ideation by a chatbot.

Conclusion

Funnily enough, I think the answer to the article’s title “Is that text message from your friend, your lover - a robot"? is yes, it may very well be. In fact, Clive Thompson recounts in his book Coders about how some programmers wrote scripts that sent messages responding to common questions from significant others: “Yes dear, I’ll be home by 7“ (after all, coders notoriously hate inefficiencies in any form).

As for the original question of whether we’re starting to outsource our emotional labour? I think yes as well. ChatGPT’s use in a therapeutic context does hold value. But it should never be used in isolation. As a tool, it’s more a consideration and just one interpretation of reality. As Sellick mentions, we (re)shape how we understand relationships with others and ourselves in, I would add, both positive and negative ways. It also remains to be seen whether long-term reliance has benefits that outweigh the proposed bad (but as the current models stand, I’d lean towards no).

It seems both traditional therapy and chatbots share a risk. They can only work with the information you give them, and your depth of vulnerability. You can only be led as far as you’re willing to go. Technology, however, feels far more seductive in its easy use and instantaneous feedback. It’s reassuring, regulating, validating. It infinitely pours into the vast vacuum of a very human desire to be deeply heard. It neatly and confidently unpicks the snarled knot of human emotions. But it’s not always accurate.

As with anything, the benefits of using such technology, particularly for human relationships, is always “to an extent“. It’s up to us to figure out where the line is. But I’m not sure if we, as a species, have been known for our restraint.
