Book Notes - Steal Like An Artist (Kleon, 2012)

how to properly steal

 31 May '26

Summary Notes

1. Steal like an artist

  • There's no such thing as an original idea -- There are no original ideas in the world. Nine times out of ten, when someone says something is original it's because they don't know the original references or sources (Jonathan Letham).
  • Be a collector, not a hoarder -- School and education don't always overlap. So it's up to you to find your teachers - whatever resonates with you, your imagination, and speaks directly to your soul.
    • Practice by copying your heroes to find your style and voice. Don't just copy their style, but imitate the thinking behind the style.
    • Keep a "morgue file" of your inspirations i.e. file of dead things you'll later reanimate in your work.
    • Keep a "praise file" for the low moments.
    • "Collect books, even if you don't plan on reading them right away. Nothing is more important than an unread library"
    • Find your creative lineage to help you make your own stuff.
      • Copy and practice pep
      • "The great thing about dead or remote masters is that they can't refuse you as an apprentice. You can learn whatever you want from them. They left their lesson plans in their work. Keep a "morgue file" (from newspaper reporters) of your influences."

An artist is a collector, not a hoarder. Hoarders collect indiscriminately, artists collect selectively and only the things they really love.

2. Don't wait until you know who you are to get started

  • It's the act of making things and doing our work that we figure out who we are. Or fake it 'til you make it i.e. pretend to be making something until you actually make something. Write the things you want to read, make the things you want to see.
  • There's a difference between imitation (merely copying the style) to emulation (translating your hero's style into your own voice). "Bad theft" is the former - you degrade, skim, steal only from one, plagiarise and pass off another's work as your own, rip off. With "good theft" you honour, study, steal from many, credit the original, transform and remix ideas.
    • Kobe Bryant copied the moves of his heroes but he couldn't completely pull them off because he had a different body type, so he had to adapt.
    • Conan O'Brien "It is our failure to become our perceived ideal that ultimately defines us and makes us unique"

A wonderful flaw about human beings is that we're incapable of making perfect copies. Our failure to copy our heroes is where we discover where our own thing lives...Add something to the world that only you can add.

3. Use your hands

  • The screen makes knowledge work so abstract. We need to embody our craft with our hands and body, not just our brain. Computers are good for editing, getting ideas ready for publishing but not good at generating.
  • Keep all your passions in your life
    • There are too many chances for us to hit the delete key and throw parts of ourselves away. Just keep spending time with your hobbies if they don't seem to connect. "Let them talk to each other. Something will begin to happen" - Tomlinson.
    • Don't worry about unity, what unifies your work is the fact that you made it, one day you'll look back and it'll all make sense
    • Bounce between projects and side hobbies. One project can inform the other. Do it because it makes you happy.
  • Take time to be bored. "Creative people need time to just sit around and do nothing."

"You can't connect the dots looking forward, you can only connect them backwards" - Steve Jobs

4. Do good work, and share it with people

  • When you're starting out, obscurity is good. There's no pressure, no distractions from getting better. Build your own world, surround yourself with things you love. You don't have to live anywhere but here.

"It isn't necessary that you leave home. Sit at your desk and listen. Don't even listen, just wait. Don't wait, be still and alone. The whole world will offer itself to you." - Franz Kafka

  • But you have to leave home at least once. Where we choose to live will have a huge impact on the work you do. It helps to live around interesting people, and not necessarily people who do what you do. If you ever find that you're the most talented person in the room, you need to find another room

You will need: curiosity, kindness, stamina, a willingness to look stupid.

  • Doing good work is tough - there are no shortcuts. Just making stuff every day, knowing you're going to suck, failing, then getting better.
  • Share with people
    • Step 1 - wonder at something. You don't even have to have anything to say. The Internet can be a place for developing work
    • Step 2 - invite people to wonder with you. The more open you are to sharing your passions, the more you learn. Allow people to get a glimpse into your process.
  • Be nice to people and make friends.

"it's not that people are mean or cruel, they're just busy" - Steven Pressfield

  • You have no control over how people will interpret your work. "Not everybody will get it." But be too busy with your work to care. Anger is a useful emotion to channel into your writing and drawing.

"Complain about the way other people make software by making software" - Andre Torrez

5. Be boring

  • Take care of your health, your wealth, and your time. Don't waste your creative on useless stuff. Amassing a body of work requires effort over time. Like the sailors of old, keep a logbook of everything you've done, where your ship has been

"Be regular and orderly in your life, so that you may be violent and original in your work" - Gustave Flaubert

  • Keep your day job until you're lucky to make a living doing what you truly love. Yes it'll take time away from you, but it'll give you a daily routine to help you schedule in time for your creative work, freedom from financial stress, other humans to steal from.

Inertia is the death of creativity. You have to stay in the groove...It's going to suck until you get back into the flow.

  • Marry well. This is the most important decision you'll ever make. A good partner keeps you grounded.

6. Creativity is constraint

  • Choosing what to leave out is crucial.
    • Those who will get ahead in the time of digital abundance are those who know what to leave out to help them focus on what's truly important.
    • Constraints can help with creative blocks. Limits are liberating for creative work.
  • Dr Seuss wrote Cat in the Hat with only 236 different words. His editor bet him he couldn't write a book with only 50 different words. He wrote Green Eggs and Ham, one of the bestselling children's books in the world

"What we respond to in any work of art is the artist's struggle against his or her limitations" - Saul Steinberg

  • What makes humans interesting is what we have experienced, and what we have not. Embrace your limitations, and keep moving.
