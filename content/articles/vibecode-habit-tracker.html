May 2026 Project: commit/

vibecoding a habit-tracking app

 20 May '26

If you've ever tried using apps to track habits, you'll know what I mean when I say that checklists are addictive. The fun animation of confetti or "Congratulations!!!" after ticking off "Get toothpaste" feels validating. Surveying a long list of "done" items makes me feel like dusting my hands off and stepping back with satisfaction like: "ahh it ain't much, but it's honest work".

You might also know what I mean when I say there was something missing when it came to apps. I wasn't able to successfully build habits. And there's a subtly different thing than purely tracking them.

The idea of habit tracking leading to habit formation rests on surfacing awareness of 'things to be done'. But in the habit-tracking apps I've tried (Todoist, Apple Notes), there were three main challenges:

  • Flat = There were at most two levels where you could group tasks into - the tasks themselves, and maybe separate folder to sort tasks into.
  • Static = The list is stable for only the tasks that are important right now, or in the near future.
  • Reactive = The flat, static structure always lead to a feeling of reactivity. You were crossing off items, but there was no space for looking ahead or identifying where it fit into values. Lists were something you pulled out when a deadline loomed, not when you were looking at a 5 year plan.

It made me feel like I was travelling overseas, but with only a checklist in hand. I was at the behest of my whims, filled with internal spontaneity and only pushed to urgency when there was some sense of external deadline. But it didn't help me to anticipate my metaphorical money running out, or if I wanted to book an experience. I needed to plan for those things.

I realised tracking habits is only the surface. It's also tracking values, exploring new things, catching up with old friends, and all the other little things that make a life. Architecture is crucial, and to have architecture is to build a system. Life is complex and full of interrelated parts.

Two concepts:

  1. August Bradley's Pipelines-Pillars-Vaults (PPV)
  2. GitHub

August Bradley's PPV system (Pillars, Pipelines, Vaults), less an app and more of a "Life OS". He uses Notion to build a dynamic system that flows between abstract, lifelong goals and the practical minutia of daily tasks. Because you can see goals at every level, you can proactively plan for the future. Each task ladders up into their respective projects, goal outcomes, value goals and pillars i.e. the foundations of your life. That way you execute towards your important long-term goals, while course-correcting with regular review cycles. I loved the idea of looking at your life as a holistic system. But, it never stuck for me. It was much easier to put off reviews, kick the task can down the road, and stare at my ever-growing "some-day" bucket list.

In terms of GitHub, I only started it using it properly a year ago when I was trying to build the first version of my website. It was an overengineered monstrosity. But, I was surprised by how much I actually enjoyed looking at my commit numbers, and seeing the heatmap fill up with little green squares. It was the same surveying feeling of looking at items marked "done". I tried to make a point of pushing something daily, even if it was terrible. Today I use a simplified and scaled down version of PPV. But I wanted to see if I could combine GitHub and PPV into a single system. Hence "commit/", a habit-tracking app that brings together the motivation of keeping GitHub streaks alive via heatmaps while maintaining Bradley's comprehensive life design philosophy.

Design Decisions

Three themes drove my design decisions:

  1. Honest self-accounting
  2. Intentional friction
  3. Emergent design features

Honest self-accounting

The temptation to kick tasks down the road was one of the biggest things that made traditional habit-tracking methods ineffective for me. I could scribble out today's goal and add it tomorrow on a whim, or it would sit collecting dust if it wasn't urgent. There were three ways I tried to solve for this:

1/ GitHub Heatmaps

Streaks are not unfamiliar to the avid habit-tracking app user. GitHub's heatmap offers immediate feedback both for tasks done (or not), and higher commit activity. However, it doesn't have an ability to filter for project-specific commits, presumably because it's optimising for other complex features. Conversely, Notion doesn't have a native heatmap feature but it can drill into minute specifics of your projects (there is a similar GitHub feature implemented by a community member though https://www.reddit.com/r/Notion/comments/1pjrczh/someone_asked_a_few_weeks_ago_if_a_githubstyle/).

Bringing this to commit/, I included both a local (project-specific) and global (all projects) as an overview. In this way, the user can see a visual indicator of their current progress on two different levels.

2/ Immutable Commits

Given the decentralised nature of GitHub i.e. anyone can contribute to projects at any time from anywhere, commits and commit hashes arose from ensuring data security and that the data wasn't tampered with. The public can interrogate and view the before and after of a piece of code as well to ensure accountability. Sure, you can revert it, but that action is recorded too.

When this lens of permanence and timestamped behaviour is translated to tasks, it changes your relationship to an object. In Notion, I could easily toggle a task on or off, unable to see that this seemingly small change had an impact on overall activity.

This was a useful feature to bring to commit/, enabling a user to view the contribution graph at both the local and global heatmap levels.

3/ In-built reviews

Although reviews are the compass for correction, it can be difficult to carve out the time to reflect. Not only that but it's challenging to set up systems for adequate data capture, compilation and analysis. There was a period of time where I faithfully tracked my time on every project I was doing, but it was only the occasional review that gave me insight into where exactly it went.

To encourage this, a short summary of stats is autogenerated in each daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly and yearly cell. It the user is unable to review the more comprehensive stat screen, a quick journal entry will hopefully be more accessible

Intentional Friction

Many habit-tracking apps have flexibility - the ability to freely toggle tasks as done or not, or start a project (but never finishing it). Therefore introducing friction intentionally was a key decision.

1/ Unidirectional Assignment

To avoid facilitating trigger-happy activations, a user can only add new items further up the chain rather than in the same level. i.e. an active project can only be active if you're in backlog first to subtly remind you of how many other projects there are.

2/ Active Projects must have due dates

A project can only be activated if it has a due date, otherwise it will be left as backlog. Making a project active is therefore a decision to commit to completing it.

3/ Avoiding unnecessary friction

However, friction should only be added where it's helpful rather than a hinderance. I had Initially required the user to go back to projects page to select a task but found that was too much friction that it was annoying.

Emergent Design Decisions

There were a few emergent design choices that added to the experience:

1) Colour Palette

GitHub already has a recognisable colour palette. I wanted to preserve the minimal concept and if there were new colours, each would be quickly scannable:

  • Scheduled tasks (purple) = these appear in both the project local and global heatmap so upcoming events are visible and easily accessed.
  • Recurring daily tasks (deep blue) = This indicates which tasks are recurring.
  • Late tasks (light blue) = If a user makes commits past their self-appointed due date, the colour changes from green to blue. The deepening colour schematic also does not appear for late entries. The app doesn't punish the user by blocking them from making commits, but it doesn't have the same subtle reward loop either.

The colour scheme was also flipped for journal entries i.e. the earlier the user submitted their reflection, the deeper the colour whereas the later they were the lighter the colour.

2) Renaming "Projects" to "Repos"

During testing I discovered that "monthly calls with friend" wasn't really a project to be completed. August Bradley notes that sometimes projects are to be maintained but not necessarily completed. It can be a habit, ritual or practice. Instead, I used the broader term of "repos" to indicate that these are domains that can either be a project or an area of life.

Claude Collaboration

There were interesting points in the journey where Claude would push back on things I'd suggested. The four more memorable ones were:

1) Design reconsideration

Given how important I considered completing reviews, I had initially thought of 3 layers of review reminders. The user would be able to configure these in Settings:

  • Remind Once = A banner would drop from the top. When viewed once it disappears.
  • Persistent Reminder = The banner remains until the user writes a review.
  • Screen Overlay = The entire stat screen gets blurred until the user writes a review.

Claude cautioned me against using a screen overlay, asking me to consider a persistent banner instead, and a hard block of editing a weekly review until after submission:

Your instinct is philosophically consistent with the commit model — and I think it's right— but the implementation detail matters. The risk isn't that people will cheat. It's that life genuinely gets in the way...If the app hard-blocks them, the most likely outcome isn't that they write it on time next week — it's that they feel the app is punishing them and disengage.

I was surprised by this gentle, empathetic pushback from Claude. I was inclined to agree - some options do feel like punishments while others are invitations. However, I would still say that the user's autonomy is still being respected because the user is self-selecting into the mode. Nevertheless, this reconsideration led to a "late entry" feature, where the review can still be submitted but the cell indicates it's late, as well as a change to a "Review once" banner only.

2) Scheduled Tasks

My vision of a scheduled task is that it would appear as a purple filled cell rather than green. However I hadn't specified it in my prompt.

When Claude coded it, it appeared as a small dot in the bottom right-hand corner of the cell. I hadn't considered such an elegant solution. A full purple square would've unnecessarily drawn the eye. As a dot, it was visible enough to attract attention, but not so loud that it overwhelmed the original colour palette.

3) Stat Screen

Claude Code assisted as a jumping off point for the stats screen. I had initially thought of the stats page as purely numbers but it suggested a visual representation instead. The immediate visual feedback helps the brain learn faster when cause and effect are tightly linked. Line graphs, bar charts and scatter plots enable concrete tracking over relative progress over time i.e. metrics of interest for analytical thinkers who enjoy understanding insights, trajectories and trends not just current state.

Claude did caution against over-tracking backfiring, fuelling negative emotions such as anxiety, obsession and perfectionism where the focus is on the numbers and not the actual project outcome (particularly for the cohort of people who find GitHub as metaphorically appealing as I do, I don't think they need more of that).

4) Time Horizons

An interesting concept was how different time horizons allowed for different lenses of analysis, a different questions, and why each time period is necessary to review when the development of habits.

  • Weekly = execution You can tactically adjust behaviours when you notice any daily deviations in data.
  • Monthly = consistency This is where you begin to recognise patterns across data points.
  • Quarterly = project accountability Across three months is when you can notice trend lines and behaviours that emerge over a longer period of time.
  • Yearly = identity The ultimate bird's eye view. Claude put it well "Yearly is identity-level. Not 'Did I go to the gym this week?' but 'Am I someone who trains consistently?'

Weekly and monthly views are more execution-based and helpful for habit tasks, while quarterly and yearly are useful for project-level metrics.

Critiques

Like with any system, there are ways to game it, and this app is not immune. GitHub does a better job where you can only push a commit to your remote repo after you've done the work. The panopticon of the Internet also keeps you accountable if you have a public repo because a curious person can interrogate your work and/or any trash commits.

There were cons in fully committing to either GitHub or Bradley's design. My initial idea of including git branches and a SourceTree-like diagram didn't fit well with Bradley's flow while Bradley's idea of value goals and goal outcomes felt to nebulous to concretise into Git vocabulary. I'd considered including hour-by-hour tracking, but it felt too granular for what was needed. Some things had to give so that the app could structurally support a project that seamlessly bridged both Git and PPV ideas.

So the scope of the app is mostly executional aspects of work (projects, milestones and tasks) only loosely guided by overarching pillars/portfolios.

What I learned

It was new programming with Claude Code - I'm glad I had some grounding by experiencing 'traditional' coding beforehand.

  1. Feelings of impatience and comparison - Having previously programmed my own website in two months or so, it was remarkable to see that I had scaffolded my app in half an hour. Even more galling, after hearing other vibecoders managing to finish a project in 8 hours, I felt like I was taking way too long. I became impatient with myself and Claude.
  2. There is a gap between building, learning and understanding - The feeling of impatience pushed me to use web Claude as an intermediary for my prompts. At the start the purpose was to learn how to prompt better, but it became all to easy to pass off the hard work to Claude.
  3. Trusting all of Claude's actions - A big security red flag. In the beginning I looked at every single line. But after a while it became easier to simply tap "Yes" for every code it'll execute.

There definitely was a slippery slope here. The biggest thing was the first point - it was wild to experience the feelings of frustration and impatience for myself after hearing about it from other people. And over some app that was meant to be a fun side project.

I fully recognise the app is quite niche. Users would need a basic understanding of Git for the extended metaphor to make sense, and even fewer may find such an idea makes sense. But I enjoyed the task of pairing the commit system and heatmapping for execution with Bradley's project management for broader life architecture and meaning. It became a lightweight version of both.
