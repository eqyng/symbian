Pseudonymity and Authenticity

the question of authenticity

Last Tended: 16.11.21

This essay is 1 of 7 essays for The Tech Progressive Writing Challenge.

What is Pseudonymity?

The online world is full of potential. Putting your real world name online enables you to connect with others but leaves you open to social attacks or technological hacks. Remaining completely anonymous affords watertight privacy but may be difficult to build a community. Pseudonymity, however, seems a happy medium on the spectrum. Balaji Srinivisan poses 2 benefits of pseudonymity:

  1. Less discrimination, more privacy
    • Pseudonymous accounts better circumvent the possibility for discrimination by making identity markers such as age, gender, race completely optional. Your username can have social reputation attached to it, but gives away less trackable data that could deal real world damage. We can fractionalise identities into “earning, speaking and real names” to facilitate the right nuance of privacy and distribution that best suits personal needs.
  2. Freedom after speech
    • To have an online profile is like setting up your own soapbox (alongside everyone else’s) in the middle of the square. On more combustible topics, you can be subject to “social media mob” chaos. Because digital reputation is a lot more viscous with far-reaching networks, the expression of a controversial idea can disrupt your “social supply chain” if negative press catches wind of it. Pseudonymity allows for the freedom to add another perspective without the social implosion

The Question of Authenticity

Would pseudonymous accounts mean that people are less accountable to their actions? This perspective has a point — people who voice their opinions online know they stake their reputation and relevance, precious commodities. For social networks of web2, online profiles were rooted in, and treated as an extension of, the real world. It was a way to interact and connect with friends or acquaintances. It tracked your life and your past was tied to your present in timestamps. We want to know that a real human is on the other side. For some reason, it’s not so appealing to know you’re following a bot who occasionally tweets pithy quotes into the void. Privacy concerns were only emergent issues.

But perhaps authenticity is defined differently in this era. With less of a need to protect your reputation you can afford to be more fearless and experimental. What really matters is the value of the work you output. The Internet’s next step into Web3 is also the next iteration in culture. It’s a culture of pseudonymity and community (it’s not unusual to now interact with accounts who have ape artwork profile pictures). As Balaji notes, the changing context facilitating pseudonymity like the economy being flipped to online transactions and exchanges. Communities such as DAOs are weighted towards the greater collective benefit which would make it difficult for bad actors to participate as easily.

Of course, Web3 will no doubt have concerns native to such a decentralised community. But there’s freedom, flexibility and even graciousness in identity nuance.
