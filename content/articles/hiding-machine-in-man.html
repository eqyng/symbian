Hiding the Machine in the Man

what a shower call revealed

 22 Aug '26

"I'm gonna go shower now. Bye."

A sentence that ended a call. Familiar, casual, quotidian.

But the conversation parties started chatting just 30 minutes ago. And only one of them was human.

This was a transcript snippet of a voice agent I helped build and maintain for a telecommunications client. The customer had initially called to troubleshoot a phone issue. Then while the agent walked them through the process, they started chatting to the bot out of boredom. By the end of the call, it seemed like the customer felt comfortable enough to mention their routine.

My team had chuckled. But a sense of discomfort lingered. "I...kind of feel bad for them", someone ventured.

The AI had done its job. It helped solve the customer's query. It was charming, helpful and witty in the right places to the right degree - as per design. The next time the customer called, the AI would not remember them due to our data retention policies.

But the customer seemed to have formed a bond with the AI. Not per design. Unexpected, actually. We thought it was enough to declare upfront that this agent was an AI. We didn't account for a complicated twang of guilt as facilitators of this unrequited connection, however fleeting.

Negotiating Contracts

In our line of work, an AI agent's disclaimer appears at the beginning of the conversation, and when the question is deemed too complex or sensitive (think bereavement or fraud), the customer is handed off to an actual human. But the design brief is always the same: Make the AI agent sound and operate as naturally as possible. Anything that resembled otherwise - taking too long to respond, misunderstanding common words, revealing its tools, a robotic cadence - was a failure and buffed out. In other words, human-like in every way but in name.

I can't imagine telling a human call centre employee I was going to make lunch. Does this make our experiences with other humans transactional? I don't think so.

I believe it speaks more to the norms and expectations we have developed around different scenarios. In the shower case, we have similar 'liminal' interactions - booking a doctor's appointment, checking a hotel room's availability, calling for help with Internet connection. The social contract is along the lines of: "I can call this number during these hours, I can expect someone knowledgable to help me reasonably quickly, then I can get on with my day". Anything else would be strange, a little off-script.

Call centres were introduced in the 1950s - 1970s to manage high volumes of telephone calls via a large centralised organisation. We are now accounting for volumes of calls that are even higher still. But the social contract with non-human entities has yet to be fully developed. The only reason we find the shower admission strange is because we're comparing the interaction to our already formed human-to-human blueprint we've learned through culture. It feels familiar, but it isn't really.

We are still negotiating the terms and conditions, transitioning between new social contracts and old. The downstream impacts of these are that we no longer know what the expected kind of behaviour is. We do not know when an action has crossed over into a violation until too late.

Magic Psychology

You enter a darkened theatre. The low murmurs of excitement from the crowd are dampened by thick, velvet curtains draping the stage. Shuffling along the aisle, you reach your seat and sink into the weathered, wine-coloured fabric. You stare up at the stage, wide-eyed in anticipation.

Prepare to be amazed!

When we watch magic shows or experience magic tricks, we expect illusion and for our senses to be deceived. We can see reality being bent, but feel curiosity and wonder how it's done. Conversely, when we watch a video only to find out in the comments it was AI-generated, or when we find out a customer service agent was AI, we feel betrayal, disappointment, embarrassment, perhaps anger. "I was tricked!" people cry.

The difference between these two scenarios is that with the first, we enter the situation voluntarily. We understand we will be enchanted in exchange for our suspension of disbelief. When the contract is fulfilled, our response is awe. Like a good joke, the frame is shared.

With the second, social media had been the birth place of lived experience and personal accounts. As such, we expect videos and images to be real, finding fragments of ourselves in other humans. But when we were genuinely moved by or laughed at an AI video, the thread of connection collapses. What were we relating to when it was fake? What does that say about us?

These self-evaluative emotions such as shame and guilt are critical in building our sense of self as individuals and as part of society (Salice et al., 2022). If we are unable to measure ourselves against expectations (i.e. is the norm having the ability to discern AI videos from human-created ones yet?) we do not feel a sense of place. We have become an outsider.

However, neither do we yet have an avenue for recourse since not only does discernment take time and exposure, but AI technology is becoming increasingly sophisticated. Not to mention malicious actors who are intentionally producing misleading videos for personal profits or otherwise. Our ability to distinguish real and fake is called into question. And so too perhaps is our ability to trust ourselves and others.

This has been a real discovery for me. As a conversation designer, I'm faced with this murky territory each time.

Our job involves trying to hide the machine-in-the-man instead of revealing the man-in-the-machine. We toe the line between human 'enough', but not so much that it becomes uncanny - like magicians of emotion. The experience of connection is unplanned and emerges from design choices. My design choices.

The customer who mentioned their shower was unexpected. Our design was accounting for malicious attackers, bored customers, but not for the one who accepted and leaned in.

I question the long-term consequences. How many of these invisible social contracts are we negotiating? Do we even know what they are? How will they change over time in terms of what norms we can expect from others, AIs, ourselves?
