Feb 2026 Project: Respecting Nature

initial experience of Nature

 21 Feb '26

I don't think I've ever been in a 'life-threatening' situation before.

I've yet to break even a bone.

Watching a horror movie or getting clammy at the prospect of public speaking or getting in middle of a cypher was about it for me.

But never a full-blown panic.

Earlier this year, I went to Japan for the snow season. A bunch of friends and I decided to try the Hirafu Peak Gates at Annupuri Mountain.

Our circle considered it was doable, nothing too crazy or 'proper' backcountry since it was lift-access off-piste. I shrugged and came along because everyone else was doing it (the first red flags of many, by the way).

It was a fair few of us, maybe six snowboarders and 2 skiiers. Three of us were new-ish to snowboarding, okay to get down the usual groomed runs and maybe a black or two with some struggle. One of crew was fine with leading us since they had avalanche safety equipment, and completed the route before.

Our group got split up between the skiiers and snowboarders due to the length of the queue for lifts. As soon as we got off the infamous "Pizza Box" chair lift (a square, single-person chair lift as large as a pizza box), the weather turned. What was meant to be a blue bird day became 50-70km/h winds (as we found out later) with low visibility. The climb up to the summit became icy, and snowboards became sails, catching in the wind. Having no prior outdoor experience really, it felt like the first time I really saw the fickleness of Nature.

At the top of the summit wasn't the end though. There was meant to be another climb to another mountaintop a little further on. Two ski guides joined our party, having backtracked as they lost their way due to the low visibility. And thank goodness they did too.

Later down the path, there was a little ledge on the mountain side that required us to climb down, then traverse a little ways across the slope. The two ski guides and our group lead were already ahead, discussing which path to take. I had taken off my snowboard, and proceeded to awkwardly get down the ledge. Somehow I slipped off.

Landing face down on my snowboard, I started to speed down the mountainside. All I could do was scrabble at the unforgiving compact surface. Everything was white white white, feeling the ground rushing away underneath me. My stomach hollowed, my heart constricted. How much runway did I have?

The ski guides apparently jumped into action, pelting down the mountain after me. One of them managed to arrest my slide, placing themself between me and the rest of the slope. I sat up, shivering. They placed a hand on my arm, introducing their name, asking for mine, reassuring tones. I couldn't speak. Simply huddled while the wind blew around us for five minutes.

Eventually we got back up the mountain. There was nothing to do but continue onwards. The valley which we had eventually ended up in was beautiful. Quiet settled amongst the trees, and you could see the ocean in the distance. The sun had come out and cast itself on pockets of powdery snow, ice crystals glinting off the surface. I was too fatigued to do anything but struggle forward though to fully appreciate it.

Afterwards we had many, many group chats discussing what went right, what went wrong, what made a good or bad judgement call. We had differing opinions on the severity of what happened, "no one would've died, maybe badly hurt".

A few reflections from that:

  • Safety awareness and asking questions - It didn't actually cross my mind to ask more questions about the route, and didn't realise it would be different to a groomed run. Also thought it would be fine because other people seemed chill with it (but that's probably just taking social cues from a crowd, not the best thing).
  • Being able to extrapolate tiredness / fatigue levels - how would you know "if I feel this tired at this point, how tired might I feel later on?"
  • Stubbornness and making a call on when to quit - Sometimes stubbornness as a trait can be helpful but not when it comes at a risk / danger. If I can't carry my own gear that's probably a good indicator that I should be turning back at some point.
  • Keeping composed in stressful situations - probably needs exposure to similar circumstances.

After returning home, I read Deep Survival by Laurence Gonzalez. I wouldn't discount going off-piste again. But I'd be more prepared for sure.
