Feb 2026 Project: Respecting Nature

initial experience of Nature

 21 Feb '26

I don't think I've ever been in a 'life-threatening' situation before.

I've yet to even break a bone.

The height of fear may be watching a horror movie or getting clammy at the prospect of public speaking or getting in the middle of a cypher.

That's about it for me. Never a full-blown panic.

Earlier this year, I went to Japan for the snow season. A bunch of my friends decided to try the Hirafu Peak Gates at Annupuri Mountain. Our circle considered it was doable, nothing too crazy or 'proper' backcountry since it was lift-access off-piste. And it was beautifully sunny in the morning, a misty blue with streaked by pale 7am sunlight. Buoyed by the energy of good weather, everything seemed possible.

The group had six snowboarders and 2 skiiers. Three of us were completely new to backcountry, but one of our crew volunteered to lead us since they had avalanche safety equipment, and completed the route before. But we hadn't discussed anything - not skill levels, route planning, or safety gear. It was only the briefest of conversations: chair lift up, hike to the summit, then skii down the other side of the mountain into a valley and meet at the cafe at the base. I had initially not planned to go (because climbing a summit sounded pretty hectic to me), but I took my cue from how nonchalant more experienced people in my group were. Maybe it was like any other run, I rationalised to myself (the first red flags of many).

The trip was already off to a poor start. The queue for the chairlifts was long and crowded, so our group got split up, and the skiiers got separated from us. We assumed they would've joined us at some point after we had gotten off the infamous "Pizza Box" chair lift, but reception was spotty. It was an experience, trundling up to a mid-point on the mountain, sitting on a single-person chair lift as large as a pizza box. By then the wind had picked up, the lift stopping at a few points to wait for the wind to die at bit as a standard safety precaution.

Now faced with the hike up, the weather properly turned. It became grey and blustery, and only got worse the further up we climbed in the increasingly icy mountain-side. 50-70km/h winds (we found out later), low visibility, snowboards catching in the wind like sails. Having had no truly outdoor experiences before, I felt like I really saw the fickleness of Nature for the first time. By then, we all felt like we had already gone 'too far' to turn back.

The top of the hike wasn't the end though. The actual summit was further along than I'd realised. We met with two ski guides who had also gotten confused, backtracking due to the low visibility. They decided to join our party, and thank goodness they did too.

Later on there was a little ledge on the mountainside that needed us to climb down, then traverse a little ways across the slope. It wasn't the steepest drop, but it was still awkward to shuffle down with a snowboard. The ski guides and our group lead were already ahead, leaning into the mountain to keep themselves steady, discussing which direction to take. I'd taken off my snowboard, to try and get down the lip. But I slipped.

Landing face down on my snowboard, the ground rushed away underneath me. I couldn't see or think about anything but white white white. I was speeding down the mountain, and all I could do was scrabble at the unforgiving compact surface. My stomach hollowed, my chest constricted. How much runway did I have?

Suddenly I stopped. One of the ski guides immediately jumped after me and arrested my slide, placing themself between me and the rest of the slope. I sat up, shivering. They held my elbow gently, introducing themselves, saying their name, asking for mine, reassuring tones. I couldn't speak. We stayed like that for five minutes, in the bubble of their warm calmness as the wind whipped around us.

Eventually we rejoined the others. There was nothing to do but continue onwards.

The valley which we had eventually ended up in was beautiful. Quiet settled amongst the trees, and you could see the ocean in the distance. The sun had come out and cast itself on pockets of powdery snow, ice crystals glinting off the surface. I was too fatigued though to fully appreciate it.

Afterwards we had many, many group chats discussing what went right, what went wrong, what made a good or bad judgement call. We had differing opinions on the severity of what happened, "No one would've died, maybe badly hurt". The skiiers had run into a few problems of their own, and were forced to turn back before reaching the summit where we were because the weather became incredibly difficult.

A few reflections from that:

  • Ask questions - Make sure you ask questions about the route so that you know the path for yourself. Beware of taking social cues from the group and substituting group confidence for individual awareness.
  • Discussing everyone's tolerance for risk and skill level. Understand where on the safety spectrum they sit, and make sure the route is actually scoped for ability. Stress test assumptions - What would happen if the weather turned? What would happen if one of us had gotten injured? What would trigger a decision to turn back? These were questions only asked after the fact.
  • Know when to call it quits. There were harms that accumulated invisibly - social pressure to continue, fatigue, wind, low visibility, split group but no communications. The ledge was the breakpoint. Understand how to monitor and extrapolate tiredness / fatigue levels i.e. if you were to ask yourself, "if I feel this tired at this point, how tired might I feel later on?"
  • Stubbornness - Sometimes stubbornness as a trait can be helpful but not when it comes at a risk / danger. If I can't carry my own gear that's probably a good indicator that I should be turning back at some point.
  • Keeping composed in stressful situations - probably needs exposure to similar circumstances.

What mattered was the ski guides who had enough embodied knowledge that their instinct to act overrode any hesitation. We had group lead who had the formal safety equipment but possibly hadn't trained in enough situations to use it. The guides' calmness in the moment stayed with me for a while afterwards. We didn't cross paths again, but I've yet to forget them.

After returning home, I researched backcountry snowboarding, and read Deep Survival by Laurence Gonzalez. This book articulated why even the most wilderness savvy person can make clearly bad decisions, and how to get through a situation that pushes you to the very limits. I was also struck by how much the experience I had applied to more situations than just mountains. The invisible accumulation of stressful factors, the eventual breakpoint, the lack of proactive planning before an incident occurred. This was a safety mishap made real.

I wouldn't discount going off-piste again. But I'd be more prepared for sure. Now I feel better equipped not just by knowing the practical types of safety equipment needed but also with a healthier dose of respect for Nature, a desire to learn more about how to work with it instead of naively against, and the first-hand experience of how powerful human connection is in a terrifying situation.
