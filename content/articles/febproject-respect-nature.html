Feb 2026 Project: Respecting Nature

initial experience of Nature

 21 Feb '26

I don't think I've ever been in a 'life-threatening' situation before.

I've yet to even break a bone.

The height of fear may be watching a horror movie or getting clammy at the prospect of public speaking or getting in middle of a cypher. That's about it for me. Never a full-blown panic.

Earlier this year, I went to Japan for the snow season. A bunch of friends and I decided to try the Hirafu Peak Gates at Annupuri Mountain.

Our circle considered it was doable, nothing too crazy or 'proper' backcountry since it was lift-access off-piste. It was a fair few of us, maybe six snowboarders and 2 skiiers. Three of us were new-ish to snowboarding, okay to get down the usual groomed runs and maybe a black or two with some struggle. One of crew was fine with leading us since they had avalanche safety equipment, and completed the route before.

It was almost a last minute decision. We hadn't discussed everyone's skill levels, nor gone over the route. We just trusted the leader. I shrugged and came along because everyone else was doing it (the first red flags of many, by the way).

Our group got split up between the skiiers and snowboarders due to the length of the queue for lifts. As soon as we got off the infamous "Pizza Box" chair lift (a square, single-person chair lift as large as a pizza box), the weather turned. What was meant to be a blue bird day became 50-70km/h winds (as we found out later) with low visibility. The climb up to the summit became icy, and snowboards became sails, catching in the wind. Having no prior outdoor experience really, it felt like it was the first time I really saw the fickleness of Nature. By then, we felt like we had already gone 'too far' to turn back.

At the top of the summit wasn't the end though. There was meant to be another climb to another mountaintop a little further on. Two ski guides joined our party, having backtracked as they lost their way due to the low visibility. And thank goodness they did too.

Later down the path, there was a little ledge on the mountain side that required us to climb down, then traverse a little ways across the slope. The two ski guides and our group lead were already ahead, discussing which path to take. I had taken off my snowboard, and proceeded to awkwardly get down the ledge. Somehow I slipped off.

Landing face down on my snowboard, I started to speed down the mountainside. All I could do was scrabble at the unforgiving compact surface. Everything was white white white, feeling the ground rushing away underneath me. My stomach hollowed, my heart constricted. How much runway did I have?

The ski guides apparently jumped into action, pelting down the mountain after me. One of them managed to arrest my slide, placing themself between me and the rest of the slope. I sat up, shivering. They held my elbow gently, introducing themselves, saying their name, asking for mine, reassuring tones. I couldn't speak. We stayed like that for five minutes, their warm calmness seemed to envelop us in a bubble while the wind whipped around us.

Eventually we got back up the mountain. There was nothing to do but continue onwards.

The valley which we had eventually ended up in was beautiful. Quiet settled amongst the trees, and you could see the ocean in the distance. The sun had come out and cast itself on pockets of powdery snow, ice crystals glinting off the surface. I was too fatigued to do anything but struggle forward though to fully appreciate it.

Afterwards we had many, many group chats discussing what went right, what went wrong, what made a good or bad judgement call. We had differing opinions on the severity of what happened, "No one would've died, maybe badly hurt".

A few reflections from that:

  • Ask questions - Make sure you ask questions about the route so that you know the path for yourself. Beware of taking social cues from the group and substituting group confidence for individual awareness.
  • Discussing everyone's tolerance for risk and skill level. Understand where on the safety spectrum they sit, and make sure the route is actually scoped for ability. Stress test assumptions - What would happen if the weather turned? What would happen if one of us had gotten injured? What would trigger a decision to turn back? These were questions only asked after the fact.
  • Know when to call it quits. There were harms that accumulated invisibly - social pressure to continue, fatigue, wind, low visibility, split group but no communications. The ledge was the breakpoint. Understand how to monitor and extrapolate tiredness / fatigue levels i.e. if you were to ask yourself, "if I feel this tired at this point, how tired might I feel later on?"
  • Stubbornness - Sometimes stubbornness as a trait can be helpful but not when it comes at a risk / danger. If I can't carry my own gear that's probably a good indicator that I should be turning back at some point.
  • Keeping composed in stressful situations - probably needs exposure to similar circumstances.

What mattered was the ski guides who had enough embodied knowledge that their instinct to act overrode any hesitation. We had group lead who had the formal safety equipment but possibly hadn't trained in enough situations to use it. The guides' calmness in the moment stayed with me for a while afterwards. We didn't cross paths again, but I've yet to forget them.

After returning home, I read Deep Survival by Laurence Gonzalez. This was a book that articulated why even the most wilderness savvy person can make clearly bad decisions, and how to get through a situation that pushes you to the very limits. I was also struck by how much the experience I had applied to more situations than just mountains. The invisible accumulation of stressful factors, the eventual breakpoint, the lack of proactive planning before an incident occurred. This was a safety mishap made real.

I wouldn't discount going off-piste again. But I'd be more prepared for sure. Equipped with some first-hand experience, a healthier dose of respect for Nature, and a desire to learn more about how to work with it instead of naively against.
