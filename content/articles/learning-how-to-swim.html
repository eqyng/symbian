Learning How to Swim

standing on generational shores

 27 Oct '21

“We’re screwed if medical students are Googling treatments we could look up ourselves”

“‘my spelling is horrible now b/c I am just waiting for spell check... / frequently look up topic, get the fact and then forget it 2 days later and do the same thing...’”

“Do you have any book recommendations for my child? She’s not talking at four years old yet…“

People’s opinions about the Internet are pretty fascinating, particularly when it comes to generational differences. I haven’t yet sampled from a younger demographic, but the first two quotes were from separate conversations I had with a senior solutions architect and technologist about digital impacts on communication. The last was from when I had a brief stint working at a bookstore where the number of parents coming in to ask me similar questions was honestly surprising. So, what’s the situation?

Get off my (pixelated) lawn

The terms “Generation X, Y, Z” etc., were apparently more for ease of marketing than abstract group delineations. But when it comes to responses from different age brackets, there are very real effects.

Infants spend a lot of time asleep. The rest of the time is dedicated to learning how to use their limbs, look around at the world, babbling and pointing at things. However, with screens being more or less used as digital pacifiers in some cases, children are spending a lot of those hours passively consuming information. This is a potential cause for delays in physical and cognitive developmental milestones.

For older children and adolescents (also adults), psychological disorders and social issues come to the forefront. Fingers are often pointed to the fast-paced Internet as a factor in the apparent increase in ADHD diagnoses (whether the cases are actually increasing, there’s just an increase in reporting or kids are now being overdiagnosed and overmedicated are other stories altogether). Another interesting theory posited by an AI ethicist is that younger generations may not have the attention capacity necessary to sit down and figure out long-term problems. Asides from that, there’s the old argument that the scroll of death for a ‘dopamine fix’ creates a chronic dissatisfaction with the present.

Parents are notably absent in the digital world, a divide that’s more than just a screen’s physical barrier. They leave kids to their own devices (pun unintended), equivalent to letting children play in suburban streets until sundown. But the Internet is more like The Upside Down, and older folks are needed. As Dr Gabor Mate proposes, the world of a child whose navigational compass is directed solely by peers can resemble more of an echo chamber. In this case, it may be difficult to sift the informational wheat from chaff because there isn’t a concrete standard. The lack of dialogue isn’t helped by the sort of generational derision different groups hold for each other either.

Full disclaimer: I’ve yet to watch Stranger Things but the analogy is useful for drawing similarities in parallel dimensions, not monsters

Not to mention other convoluted knots such as mental health and phone ‘addiction’. Social beauty norms and standards, for instance, seem to have broadened with the rise in primarily visual-based social media platforms. Not only are children inclined to compare themselves to each other, but also to a wider age bracket of young adults or even older (insert reel of a woman comparing herself at 13 [pink overalls and badly applied makeup] with an observation of today’s 13 year old [blouse and a glamorous look]).

Digital Darwinism

Our 2D, analogue senses are evolving into those better suited to a digital environment. It’s an adaptation being driven by an algorithmic evolutionary pressure instead of biological. Like Galapagos finches, the changes would be slow and not so physically apparent at first. But over time, the distinctions and subtle cultural beacons would be clear.

From a cognitive perspective we’re evolving in leaps and bounds. The diagnosis of ADHD could be more a legacy label on a new type of thinking - one that’s characterised by non-linearity, abstraction, and hyperfocus. In fact, the ability to switch and optimise attention between competing geysers of information could be a survival tactic to keep pace with an ever-changing world. Phones and screens are being used as education and entertainment tools. I’m incredibly curious to see how children’s brains are being changed on a neural level. Children experience a “vocabulary explosion” from 2 to 6 years old going from speaking 100-2000 words to 5000-20 000 words. And this is presumably only data from children interacting with their parents and other children while at school. Now that they have an extremely portable and infinitely interesting dictionary in their hands, the number of words they’re being exposed to is exponential.

Doctors may be looking up details but the overarching mental models they construct through years of schooling will differentiate a collapsed lung lobe from a tumour (which look the same if it were not for years of expertise). Further, the question is why would you not offload extraneous information if you had the chance? The method of operation in the digital age isn’t the number of facts you can recite, but how you can assemble them together into a coherent story. Dr Google and all its carcinogen suggestions can’t help us (but with artificial intelligence we may get closer).

It’s not only a redefinition of the cognitive but also proprioceptive and physiological. First person shooter games for example are the poster children for training hand-eye coordination (my younger cousin’s bombastic fps games are a far cry from my sedate finding puzzles with tinkling bell sounds every time you successfully spot an item).

And we haven’t even started talking about fully immersive virtual reality games yet either. What might that mean for individuals growing up with an understanding that the world can extend beyond the material? We begin to enter dreams. As time goes on, the understanding of alternate realities will transform into an expectation of them. Beyond that, perhaps the act of psychological comparison against a broader biological measuring stick may have relatively sped up actual maturation as well.

Learning how to swim

First the Internet was likened to “drinking out of a fire hydrant” and there were “pools” of data. Now there are data streams, lakes and oceans.

In keeping to the theme of bodies of water, the current analogy I’ve thought of for the generational dialogue is a beach — older generations were born on land, younger generations are born directly in the sea. These are different terrains, different languages, different worlds. But, when the middle generation opened their eyes, they found themselves on the shoreline, water washing over their toes. Born too late, born too early perhaps. Like the family’s diplomatic, problematic middle child these are the ones we hold close. They possibly have the most responsibility in building the bridges between both.

Your age can be a vague marker of where you stand but I believe it’s more about your mentality, your openness to change and risk. We’re all embarking on a journey to reach the ocean. We’re trying to immerse ourselves in it. We’re all just learning how to swim.
