Figuring out the edges of ethics

Reflections on Stanford's Ethics for Practitioners Course

 06 Nov '26

What does "ethics" actually mean?

Ethics has a long history stemming from divine myths and Ancient Greece to modern notions of bioethics and AI. Generally speaking, it is a discipline that differentiates between 'good' and 'bad', 'right' from 'wrong'. How can we as a society agree on a shared system of rules, principles and values i.e. how can we decide how to best live?

So, one could be forgiven for thinking a course on AI ethics means concretely knowing what the right or wrong way to think about AI is. Not so for Stanford's Ethics, Technology and Public Policy for Practitioners program. Over 7 weeks, 200 tech practitioners around the globe came together to hear from leaders of both academic faculty and the field across different industries, policy and government. This course offered not just a shared examination of 'ethics' from multifaceted lenses, but provided a practical toolkit and vocabulary to share it with others.

As a practitioner, there is no doubt about the benefits of AI: the way it improves and provides insights about domains, facilitates personalised knowledge at our fingertips and enables efficient decision-making where humans are slow and error-prone.

However, we are equally conscious of the perils of AI as well. Here are the key learnings I gleaned across the several weeks.

Key Learnings:

Fiction as a tool for moral framing

Rob Reich's introduction to the course was perhaps unusual. The first session began with a story - Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas. But it was an effective teaching tool.

Fiction is a useful sandbox for moral imagination. Stories help us consider different values; to try on ideas, inspect them in the mirror and place them back on the rack if we choose. Logic and the tight grip our beliefs have on us loosens, and we enter a different world through the vehicle of a parable or fairytale. We allow ourselves to be subtly changed without confrontation. This is especially true when we discuss personal topics like morals and values, bricks that may be core to our identity and ideas we may find ourselves defending without fully knowing why.

AI occupies grey space in multiple ways

AI as a technology seeps between the gaps of society in many ways. Sometimes we can clearly pinpoint where it is 'obviously' wrong, but other times the effects are so fine, like groundwater.

We have relied on the law to constrain what we can see, but even then it may not be effective due to an outdated legal system, variable definitions or the fact that what is legal may not always be ethical. A few examples: - AI currently pushes against the limits of liability. Software was not originally considered a product under liability law since 1985 so accountable parties were not established until Europe's Product Liability Directive in 2024. - In terms of AI itself, the definition of "open source" is variable. Different parts of the model can be 'open' at different times and for different reasons. - The definition of "fairness" has not been agreed on. Some measures of fairness are not consistent or the dataset can be disproportional (for instance the data used for learning black defendants recidivism rate higher than white defendants).

Regulation versus innovation is a false dichotomy

There is a common concern that premature regulation chokes innovation (and this has led to geopolitcal characterisations of "Europe regulates, US innovates").

But a researcher brought up a great example of the automotive industry: It was because research was poured into safety features like seatbelts, airbags and brakes that enabled us to push the frontier of engineering.

The AI industry however seems to have overoptimised for capability without equal fervour for sustainability. A plant that shoots up without nutrients quickly falls in the wind.

Tradeoffs are decisions are values

Mehran Sahami started one of his lectures with the Turing Test - a thought experiment that laid out that a successful computer program was one where a human could not distinguish between human or computer. However, Erik Brynjolfsson points out that this is a "Turing Trap" - the metric of success is one where a computer replaces a human. The downstream impacts of this metric, along with infrastructure like taxes, leads to contexts that favour job automation, displacement of purpose and unemployment. The benchmark of technology does not measure human augmentation.

Likewise, ethics being defined as a practice determining 'right' and 'wrong' may give an air that there is a right or wrong answer. Rob Reich provides a new framing, where there are only better questions that elicit better answers. Even the idea of 'better' allows a mental model that moves away from a binary pattern of thinking, to one that sits on a spectrum and accounts for alternatives, objections and reasoning under stress-testing.

Rather than funnelling ourselves into only two pathways, we can consider a variety of options and examine comparisons between each. Our decisions can then be based on what we prioritise at a point in time, or what we deeply value.

Effectiveness is a delicate position

When I mention ethics to others, I often get puzzled looks and careful questions of "how does this work in practice?" Fair - people consider ethics as fluffy or locked in ivory towers of academia.

I've noticed that a point was stressed a few times by leaders in the field: autonomy is important. Particularly ideological, research and financial autonomy. To operate as a third party allows you freedom to be in conversation with big tech companies, without being beholden to them. There is space for disagreements, to leave the room when you see fit.

Likewise, there is a need for personal commitment and grassroots movement. It reminds me of the great authors, philosophers and mathematicians who were also great activists in their time (particularly after the World Wars). As another leader put it: Democracy is a technology.

Hard questions requires a shared disposition

Similar to great innovation stemming from great safety, we can only have meaningful conversations if we are willing to ask and receive challenging questions. To do so means sharing a particular disposition. Rob Reich offers the Socratic rules of engagement. - Psychological safety - this is a safe place to ask hard questions - Be willing to remain in productive discomfort - Act in a spirit of charity - Be vulnerable I noticed the leaders themselves embodied this, using collaborative language like "If I can reflect", "Can I pursue this with you?", "Let's role play".

Ultimately, it was not just the content that was invaluable, but it was also the people. Being around people who shared the same concerns I did, and also shared issues of a different flavour as influenced by their industry or culture. It expanded my worldview. The incentive to speed up is strong, but being part of a cohort that valued friction, discussion and turning back towards each other to figure out the edges was inspiring.

Ethics as a practitioner means existing in the grey spaces, fully understanding all the choices on the table, and making sure the vocabulary to speak to people better.

Let's get to work.
