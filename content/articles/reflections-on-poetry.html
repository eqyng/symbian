on writing as an art form

 14 Jun '26

I've been writing for as long as I can remember. But poetry feels like a relatively recent love.

My earliest poems were scribbled on scraps of paper, jotted down in small leather-bound notebooks or even in the final, blank pages of dollar store paperbacks. Memories I'm reflecting on after curating my first poetry collection, "Caterpillar Dreams". It's a three part piece of work that uses the lifecycle of a butterfly - molting, chrysalis and finally, eclosion - as an analogy for an individual's metamorphosis. A 'do caterpillars dream of butterflies' sort of feeling.

These poems were written over the better part of a decade, hidden away in anonymous social media accounts like lint-covered sofa money. But they all followed a theme I felt linguistically compelled to explore: How can we describe immediacy and physical sensation with the imperfect, abstract vehicle of words?

A question that proved fertile enough for discoveries.

1/ a good writer is a gracious host

Words construct a lattice of experience.

In fiction, there is an art in locating where the lattice is pliable enough to give way to abstraction and metaphor, as well as where it needs to be enforced and clear so meaning is maintained.

As the writer, you are responsible for leading your reader's experience. It requires a sensitivity to see how someone will receive a sequence of events, as if you are preparing and lighting a dark pathway for a guest. To honour the trust of a visitor who's volunteered to step into your world, you must answer questions such as:

  • How do you respect your reader's autonomy, and gift them with their own discoveries at the right moment?
  • How do you pace the journey, in what order, and toward what end?
  • Where do you ask your reader to merely observe, and when do you invite them to participate?

To rush or dawdle risks shattering the reverie. To architect is to handle with precision and care.

2/ developing a style demands decisions

In fiction, my writing voice is maximally liberated meaning-wise since I'm not required to argue a point or make a claim. I don't need to drive the work forward through persuasion or land squarely on a stance or opinion. Also without the rigour of English academia, I'm free to indulge any grammatical whim.

My usual creative process is: a phrase or an emotion lurches like a sea beast under the surface of my consciousness, and I'd reel them into reality. I don't always know what'll emerge when I do. Sometimes the poem lies fleshed out on the casting deck, undignified and gasping in the harsh light of manifestation. Other times, they write themselves as if I was merely the conduit for their existence.

Structure-wise though, the compressed art form of poetry provides the grounds I find most creatively stimulating. Constraints require workarounds, justified decisions and for each word to earn its place. Like a spelunker, I would meticulously tap and hold different words to the ear for polish and precision.

Over time, my poetry has developed two stylistic choices:

1) Their content mirrors their amorphous, undefinable origins: nameless and faceless characters, flashes of feelings, moments in time.

2) A tactility of phrasing that the reader can derive enjoyment from reading aloud.

3/ poems are another form of essay

My poetry explores the same question as my analytical essays, simply in a different medium i.e. exploring interfaces and boundaries.

For "Caterpillar Dreams" in particular, the poems are concerned with a few themes:

  • Bridging bodily sensation and intellectual meaning - It's an impossible, asymptotic exercise but the approximate meeting place is through sound architecture i.e. the physical sound and form words make when they're expressed in mind or mouth.

  • The person inside and/or outside - Nameless figures or "you"s and "I"s feature a lot in my poetry. The anonymity helps according to subject matter. For intimate subjects, there is plausible deniability where the reader may or may not participate. For intense subjects, nothing is required from the reader but observation and presence.

  • The boundary of multiple meanings - My poems are sometimes ambiguous, a subtle gesture rather than loud announcement. Since the vagueness needs to be deciphered and considered, the reader slows down, as opposed to an explicit message which is easily understood, easily interpreted and passed over.

Rather than the heady thought process that can easily spiral into pessimism, poetry moves into the body, tapping into an almost pre-verbal state that forces a grounding effect. In bypassing someone's cognition this way, preparing the conditions for someone's felt experience needs careful tending.

My poetry and essay writing are two sides of the same coin.

Where analytical essays are clear, structured and (sometimes) correct, poems are experiential with multiple meanings. The purpose becomes embodied through the senses, not just cerebral and remembered (or forgotten).

But in either case, writing is an art form that requires a level of detail on the writer's part such that the attention and meaning translates to the reader.
