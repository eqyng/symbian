Fixed on Flexible Living

the homes are alive

Last Tended: 05.10.21

A Rock and a Hard Place

Our population of 7.7 billion people (2019) has been projected to increase to 9.7 billion in 2050 but one in three people will also reach retirement age by 2050. Advances in technology and medicine have additionally led to a longevity of life and a generation squeezed between supporting both their children and parents. We therefore have overpopulation of an ageing population, more people but older people out of the workforce. These quandaries aren’t helped by housing shortages pushing up rent prices and increases in underemployment are confronting for people wishing to make significant financial decisions. And all of this is underscored by the long-term uncertainties of a pandemic we of course forgot about. No wonder current generations are all about sustainability - they inherit the earth…and the weight of our problems.

Solutions ping-pong between the obvious ‘construct more houses’, and the not-so obvious ‘have less children’ (the anti-natalist proposal which would clearly not solve the ageing population side). However, a third solution seems to have slid into discussion - architecture. We can’t solve everything but if the population issue seems to be inflexible, perhaps it’s our space that needs to be more pliable. Covid-19 has also forced different aspects of our lives into one space, areas now doubling as makeshift dining halls, offices and school rooms. For those fortunate enough to work from home, it seems almost certain that setups must remain ergonomic and tables standing. With the world evolving in general, the consideration of space is an aspect of sustainable living (eNORME studio, Domestika).

The Homes are Alive

In conventional buildings, static architecture means pockets of space being underused or used inefficiently: items half-stacked in tall-shelved cabinets, furniture pushed against the walls, and spaces siloed by doors. Further, the needs of occupants change over time, necessitating (costly) redesigns to facilitate them. However, flexible spaces have broken the mould by enabling occupants to rearrange the room as they please. This example of innovation dates as far back as Buckminster Fuller. The appeal to designers then is similar to why architects are embracing it now - an “optimisation of technology” and endeavour to use existing resources more creatively through redesign. With such practical and economic longevity, architecture can evolve over spans of time, place and cultures.

Robert Kronenburg is one of the contemporary leading voices, championing and popularising this design. An example of commercial architecture that captures the sentiment is The Matsumoto Performing Arts Centre. With mobile furniture, open design and a ceiling that can be raised or lowered to suit different types of performances, the multiple-purpose building has the ultimate capacity. Since it can suit a wide range of needs, diverse demographics are welcomed into the space, thereby turning it into a centre of activity and action.

“non-authoritative solutions to the small-space dilemma” (Aakriti, 2018)

In residential areas, a similar story unfolds. Instead of the usual blueprints of walls, there are blueprints of space meticulously deconstructed into planes and organised around the truly immovable utilities such as water pipes and electricity cables. Residences with portable, demountable and modular functions are responsive to change across the occupants’ lifetime in a socially, economically and environmentally viable fashion. Like magic, rooms are conjured out of air by playing with proportions, pulling levers and pushing walls, swiveling tables and swinging doors, all unified by colour palettes and lighting. Spaces become alive and dynamic, just like us.

A building is not a place - it simply brings a place into existence — Heidegger, 1993 (as in Kronenberg)

With the Sound of Music

There’s not just the practical component of being able to fit more into small spaces, but a whole host of other needs such as psycho-social, aesthetic, or privacy. Living spaces embody and influence the essence of its occupants. It’s no surprise that interiors impact our behaviour, emotions and perceptions - warmer colours are comforting, natural light stimulates productivity. Instead of humans conforming to the limits of four walls, the home conforms to human needs in a “people-centric” manner. Even further, an interesting proposal by Ghandi suggests that the human-computer future of architecture can attend to neurological requirements like that of PTSD patients whose ability to vocalise needs has been affected by their experiences.

The environment serves a dual purpose. Our possessions are not just objects but act as functional and emotional mirrors of who we are. Humans are not only built to adapt in the flexible style of nature, but we are equally functional and beautiful. The novelty of secrete storage holds a similar delight of children when discovering a new toy’s functions. Moveable and modular units bring an element of play into spaces where the drudgery of work now occupies. Instead of a cacophony of our furniture working against us, when our environment is aligned with our workflow a symphony and deeper sense of belonging can be attained.

NTS: Hong Kong Small Apartment - City Veil

However, it’s difficult to unpick the beginning and end of whether the movement of downsizing is one part trend and one part necessity. The zeitgeist of minimalism, holistic wellness and potted plants meets 5G networks, smart homes, and small cleaning robots. Perhaps the more we enter the technological domain, the more we fantasise about forests, until we again crave convenience. After all, is not the best type of customer service one that can predict our needs before we’re even conscious of them?

In broad brushstrokes, flexible living is a carefully designed solution that takes into account global spaces, the histories of the house and narratives of the occupants’ lifestyles. In a world that continually expands, we’re not left with many options. The flexible small home is the perfect representation of the 21st century - only as much information as we need, at the time that we need it. The busyness of everything else is redundant and tucked away out of sight. For the modern urbanite, perhaps it’s a way of navigating, and finally living in tandem with, the external and internal nature of chaos and order.
