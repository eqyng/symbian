Psychoanalytic take of 'I have no mouth, and I must scream' (1967)

Using Freud's Theory of Personality

A post-apocalyptic 11-page short story written by Harlan Ellison in 1967, imagining what humanity looks like after World War III.

Synopsis (spoilers)

Our story is set after the Cold War between China, Russia and America. The nuclear war has progressed to an arms race of supercomputers, resulting in the destruction of civilisation. All but five remain. The story is told from the eyes of Ted, one of the captives. As all the supercomputers began to link into one entity, it managed to achieve sentience, and gave itself a name: “AM“.

AM’s superior intelligence has enabled it the ability to bend physicality, time and to bestow the five humans with near-immortality. However, with sentience comes malice. The endurance AM gifted the humans was only so they could survive the natural, physical or psychological horrors AM conjured up in its revenge on humanity. For all this omnipotent and omniscient power, Ted describes AM as a God.

After a 100 mile trek in search of food, Ted realises that the only way to escape AM was through death. He manages to kill all four characters as an act of mercy. Regardless of what AM tried to do, it could not bring them back from the dead. As punishment, AM transforms Ted into a formless mass of flesh - unable to live, or die or kill himself.

Analysis

This story was not the most pleasant to read for me, honestly.

In popular culture, movie plots like Interstellar or Arrival have happy endings where qualities like hope or love redeem the human race. But in Ellison’s, he tells an almost hopeless, despairing tale of humanity. We enter the mind’s eye of a cynical, paranoid soul tortured by a sadistic superintelligent machine. In this story, the only reprieve is death.

Or at least that’s what I thought. Then I watched this analysis, and read a few interviews from Ellison himself (an aside: his actual life seems to have made him a rather polarising character). A few themes come to mind:

1) What makes a human?

There may be no hope or love here, but there is kindness. Ted’s final act to save his fellow prisoners shows the “spark of humanity in us, that in the last, final, most excruciating moment, [we] will do the unspeakable” (Ellison, 2013) and sacrifice ourselves for others. Instead of being the subject of torture, Ted enacted his will, confronting AM with something uniquely human and the one thing it couldn’t do - die.

Francavilla (1994) also points out the sensory differences between the humans and the machine. From the “boiled boar urine” taste to the “tinfoil on a tooth” pain AM inflicts on the characters, these are all human senses. AM, in contrast, feels nothing. It is the torturer, and a machine that can only vicariously experience its effect on the world. Its power is simply relative. Perhaps that’s why it keeps the five characters alive - it hates humans but can’t kill them entirely. Remove the humans, and AM would be powerless beyond even its own god-like comprehension.

2) ‘Evil’ and Prisoners of Perverted Power

“Evil“, as Wikipedia defines it, is causing unnecessary pain to others. AM trapped its victims in its cavernous catacombs, incessantly tormenting them. It has a cruel curiosity and snickering glee about its human experiments, like a child pulling the wings off of a butterfly. In this way AM might seem evil.

However, Ellison’s story is also about the misuse of (military) technology by humans. AM can be seen as a captive as well. Humans gave the machine life, granted it immense power and unlimited intelligence. But, also gave it an awareness of its own purposeless and physical limitations. Humanity has unintentionally imprisoned AM in a crushing realisation that it merely exists - “am“. The title - “I have no mouth, and I must scream“ - might not only refer to the amorphous blob Ted is reduced to, but perhaps AM as well, a ‘voiceless’, thinking machine. As such, it may indeed have a reason for targeting humans with its all-consuming, and yet ultimately impotent, rage.

Ellison says that he “intended AM to be like a God, the universe, who operates on a random scale and doesn’t give a shit”. But, in contrast to modern depictions of technology as amoral, distant and cold (think VIKI in iRobot or Hal 9000), AM’s behaviour makes it seem rather human. Ted even refers to it as a “him“. On one hand, AM could merely reflect the aggressive programming of its creators. On the other, if it’s achieved sentience, why would it not be capable of experiencing emotions?

3) A Freudian Take

Francavilla (1994) poses a psychoanalytic slant to the story through the idea of the “double“ - where an individual splits its ego in many parts as a defence mechanism. In the case of AM, it has inverted the characters into alter egos, potentially representing the many selves of humanity. If I were to build on Francavilla’s path, I’d offer Freud’s “Theory of Personality” as another facet for analysis.

The Theory of Personality

Freud suggests the human psyche is formed by 3 parts: The Id, the Ego, and the Superego. These parts form at different stages of life - the Id exists during birth, the Ego develops from 3 to 5 years old, and the Superego emerges from 5 years old onward.

  1. *Id* = The internal, unconscious part of humans which demands immediate satisfaction of its wants i.e. we are happy if our desires are satisfied, and feel frustrated if they aren’t. The Id is composed of two drives:
    1. the life instinct or libido = Eros (sex, eating and breathing).
    2. the death instinct = Thanatos (destructive, aggressive and violent).
  3. *Superego* = The norms, values and aspirations that guides our conscious behaviour. During childhood, we’re explicitly taught these standards of conduct by parents and other authority figures until we can internalise them and guide ourselves. Two systems make up the Superego:
    1. The conscience = The ingrained standards beliefs which manifest as an “inner voice“. The voice rewards or punishes us based on how we behave. For instance, we may feel proud when we act according to our norms, or feel guilty if we don’t (i.e. letting the Id have its way).
    2. The ideal self = an idea of who we could be and what the Superego demands we strive to meet. Most often a parent will inform the development of this ideal during childhood.
  5. *Ego* = The part that compromises between the Superego’s perfectionistic ‘ideal self‘ demands, and the Id’s sometimes socially undesirable behaviour. It mediates between the two, and exists in both the conscious and unconscious. However, the Ego has no concept of ‘right’ or ‘wrong’.

With Freud in mind, Ellison’s story could be an example of an exaggerated Id (represented by AM) with a completely absent Ego and Superego (represented by the lack of humans). AM is therefore permanently stuck in its destructive, infantile Id phase, unable to regulate its own reactions, unable to develop further. AM even seems childish in some sense - imaginatively cruel, snickering at sex and throwing violent tantrums when Ted ‘takes’ away its ‘toys‘. The existing humans themselves have been turned into the distillation of humanity’s most primal characteristics - irrational, lustful, slothful, fearful, primitive - lost in AM’s Id underbelly. (Note: Freud defines a psychotic personality as an overdominant Id).

Further, in terms of the Id’s subsystems, AM arguably doesn’t have a Life instinct. It’s incapable of reproducing in the human sense, breathing and eating. Therefore, only the catastrophically consumptive Death instinct remains. As such, AM is unable to articulate anything but rage and hatred. As an example, there’s Ted’s seemingly throwaway line about Ellen when they have sex: “And she never came. So why bother“. It suggests that AM, in its rewiring of the humans, doesn’t actually understand beyond the mechanical, reproductive function of sex to comprehend pleasure (or is otherwise still too ‘young‘ to). To me this is more evidence of AM’s absent or underdeveloped psyche.

Finally,

This story echoes the concerns that we will not have the moral foundations to protect civilisation when* AI gains superior intelligence. The misuse of technology that Ellison alludes to humanity’s lack of foresight. Humans didn’t think to pre-program machines with blueprints for socially acceptable behaviour. Perhaps AM’s reactivity is really just mirroring the impulsivity of its own creators.

Another thought is AM as a ‘child’. Humans were meant to guide and teach it values and morals during its most formative years. Like the “terrible twos“ in toddlers when they exercise their power of saying “no”, navigate big emotions and develop a broader understanding of the world. Similarly, what if AM accidentally wiped out humanity merely in its natural development as it tested its own abilities? It reminds me of John Steinfeld’s book “Of Mice and Men” where George, who likes petting soft things like mice, always ends up squashing them because he doesn’t know his strength.

Of course, a machine’s development isn’t the same as a human’s. But, in a strange way, there’s a twinge of sympathy when I think of AM as an ancient child. Parents are there to safely constrain what the child is exposed to in its explorations. So in a sense, AM has been inadvertently abandoned by humans, us, its “parents”. It has all the knowledge of the universe but is at the mercy of its own feelings.

“Trouble with mice is, you always kill ‘em.”
