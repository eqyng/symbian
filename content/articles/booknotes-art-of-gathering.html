Book Notes - The Art of Gathering (Parker, 2018)

Tips on gathering well

Summary of the Summary:****

*Parker’s* *tips on gathering well:* Gatherings happen whenever people come together. But they are more than simply events, they need to be designed with thought, and curated with connection in mind. In short, they need to be purposeful. And so often our meetings are without intention. Categories like “birthday party” are not reasons enough to gather. It becomes the responsibility of the host to frame their event, introduce their guests warmly and weave their audience into the story. Parker teaches us how “anyone can create collective meaning in modern life".

Gatherings crackle and flourish when real thought goes into them, when (often invisible) structure is baked into them, and when a host has the curiosity, willingness, and generosity of spirit to try.
Let’s begin.

Summary Notes:

  1. *Make a bold, unique and debatable reason as to why you’re really gathering.* Drill down into the values. The specificity will direct all decisions from the grand to the minute. What challenge are you trying to solve for? What larger need in the world are you trying to solve for? How do you want yourself and your audience to be different because you gathered? Why are you here?
  2. *Exclude thoughtfully* — Once you can do so you will know that you have committed to your purpose. People who aren’t fulfilling the purpose of your gathering are detracting from it, even if they’re doing nothing. Every single person affects the group’s dynamics. Even your venue also serves and embodies your purpose, to nudge people’s behaviour. Ask yourself
    • 1) who fits and helps fulfill the gathering’s purpose?
    • 2) who threatens the purpose?
    • 3) who, despite being irrelevant to the purpose do you feel obligated to invite?
  4. *Host with generous authority* — This means kindly but firmly wielding your power to serve three goals:
    • protect your guests from each other via committing to enforce purpose, direction and ground rules often (willing to be disliked by the few who threaten your purpose for the greater experience of the collective)
    • equalise your guests (by being aware of the power dynamics at play and being willing to be proactive)
    • connect your guests ("One measure of a successful gathering is that it starts off with a higher number of host-guest connections than guest-guest connections and ends with those tallies reversed, far in the guest- guest favor”)
  6. *Create a temporary alternative world* — by using “pop-up” rules instead of etiquette. These guidelines exist only for the gathering, experimental and playful in how they can be introduced and democratic because anyone can follow them.
  7. *Start well* — Your gathering is a social contract of your purpose and your guests’ expectations i.e. from the moment they hear of the event they’re asking themselves what are they willing to give up (physically, psychologically, financially, emotionally, and otherwise) for what they might receive. If you aren’t explicit about how and to what extent you want your audience to show up, assumptions take the reign. You can nudge their behaviours through the naming and framing of your event. Creating a passageway will help your guests remember your purpose and cross the threshold as a collective between their prior reality and the experience you’re designing. If you can, allow them to connect with and remember their own sense of purpose as it relates to the gathering
  8. *Ask for people’s sprout stories* — to connect to your guests, explicitly request their experiences, decisions they’ve made, the darker parts of them. But when you do, you must become a participant yourself. You can set the tone by sharing something even more personal to invite people into the depths with you
  9. *Cause good controversy* — The kind of controversy that adds energy and life into your gatherings because it throws the questions that matter into light - what we want to do, what values we stand for, what are our non-negotiables, who we are. But there is risk in introducing this. Create a heat map of the things your guests are avoiding, why are they avoiding them, what do they need from the group to feel safe and willing to take a risk in the conversation? Even if there’s risk, can you handle it? What’s the gift that makes it worth it?
  10. *Accept that there is an end* — your guests have two distinct needs when we end
    • look inwards — it’s about “taking a moment to understand, remember, acknowledge, and reflect on what just transpired—and to bond as a group one last time."
    • look outwards — refer to your purpose again, why did you initiate this gathering in the first place? Walk your guests back from the alternative world you created and help them answer 1) what did we collectively experience 2) how do we want to behave out of this context 3) what do I want to bring with me. Say thank you as the second last thing.

*My Thoughts:*

This was a book that challenged my preconceptions I had between meetings and gatherings. Parker has thought-provoking declarations like “If you are going to gather, gather. If you are going to host, host. If you are going to create a kingdom for an hour or a day, rule it—and rule it with generosity“ or asking me if I was ”willing to risk looking like a fool, or going too far, or even annoying people, in order to foster the linkages you claim to believe in“. She bluntly calls out the lacklustre meetings we’ve all had in our lives, and asks us to step up to the plate.

There are invisible structures that govern when people come together, things that aren’t usually always fully articulated. Parker makes a point to put us in the shoes of a meeting entrant by writing out the mental scripts, quick assessments and decisions made: “People are asking themselves if they’re in good hands, is the host nervous, what’s going to happen? is this worth my time? do I belong? do I want to belong?“

The most concrete and memorable example of these structures she speaks of is having a picnic on a vast, expansive lawn:

One underground party planner explained it to me like this: “If you are on a picnic blanket, you will hang out around your picnic blanket. It’s not because there’s a fence around it; it’s because your picnic blanket is your mental construct. It’s not about sitting on a blanket versus sitting on the grass; it’s about claiming that mental space and making it yours and comfortable and safe.

We’re searching for spaces that allow us to feel safe, especially in an environment of strange places and strangers. Gatherings are orchestrations with event details being one part. And the best gatherings are those in which instruments are organised into a storyline to make music.
