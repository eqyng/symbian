March 2026 Project: Learn sign language

communicating differently

 25 Mar '26

I'd been wanting to learn sign language for a long time.

Back when I was in university, careers fairs were held throughout the year. Company representatives stood behind rickety folding tables pushed against the walls of the Great Hall as hundreds of students stamped through, ready to hear about the working world.

Behind one such table, I saw a person signing to their conversation partner. It was the first time I saw sign language in real life. As a fairly withdrawn young adult, I was transfixed. It was because she was so animated, their incorporation of full body movement, and how amidst the noise there was a clear conversation, just communicated differently.

So when I came across an ad of an Australian Sign Language (Auslan) school opening up a new six-week term, I signed up. And I had no excuse really, it was a 3 minute walk from home.

Lessons

It was fascinating to hear why other people had joined: The stories were varied: One person had been diagnosed with progressive hearing loss and was predicted to lose their hearing in two years. A few others worked with children or were paediatricians, and noted that kids were calmer when they had different modes of communication. Others were passionate about accessibility, and this was a group of underrepresented people we all need to learn more about.

I realised how important the sense of hearing was to me: In the first week, an interpreter stood at the back of the class, translating the teacher's signs for us. I couldn't help constantly looking at them speaking, my ears then my sight drawn by the sound. (I learned later that you must look at the deaf person signing despite the translator speaking, otherwise you may come across as ignoring the person).

Deaf gain: Within the deaf community there's a concept of "deaf gain". Deaf people don't consider their deafness as a detractor, but as something that adds to the world around them. They are proud of their culture, and celebrated it. Their achievements are because of deafness rather than in spite of.

How limited our facial expressions are in normal conversation: This was something our deaf teacher would note constantly. When we were learning how to sign emotions or questions like "happy" or "sad" or "you", we had a blank face. I realised that because I can use words, I rely on them quite heavily to get my point across, and can sometimes be lazy in how I communicate.

By the end of the term, we were comfortable with signing faster and actually understanding stories the teachers told us (their physical comedy is top tier). It was a new experience to be in a completely silent room filled with people, but everyone was still 'chatting' to one another. I felt like I was stepping into a different world.

I learned a lot. I became more comfortable with moving around to express myself. I was more patient and attentive with my classmates, watching their facial expressions, signing for them to sign again when I didn't understand them.

I felt like I understood a bit about how challenging it could be to be in a world that sometimes only heard and catered for the loudest in the room.
