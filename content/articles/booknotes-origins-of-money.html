Book Notes - The Origins of Money (Menger, 1892)

how money really is a social construct

 05 Sep '21

Last Tended: 13.09.21

Summary of the Summary:

Menger’s 26 page essay: Money is a tool which we use but usually only vaguely understand - ‘I use this thing to get that thing that I want’. But how did money as a medium of exchange come to be? Media exchanges can be instated through legislation, but it’s not its primary origin. Neither is it so organic to have risen completely out of social institution. On their own, both are incomplete explanations. Menger explains that individuals within the marketplace have always been the primordial pool from which ‘value’ and the discrimination between commodities originated. However, the state has been equally important in perfecting and scaling the marketplace into an enduring, complex system. He proposes a simple foundation for the economy’s complex workings - pure habit.

Man himself is the beginning and the end of every economy — Menger

Summary Notes:

Foreword: Rather than the monetary theory system (Austrian School), Keynesian-trained economists perpetuate funding models that satisfy politicians who believe the solution to destabilised economies is money-printing. However, it’s really the marketplace that creates money, and decides its value. — Douglas E. French, 2009

I. Introduction

What is the function of money, and why is it such a universal current medium of exchange? Did money just come about naturally?

Why is that the economic man is ready to accept a certain kind of commodity, even if he does not need it, or if his need of it is already supplied, in exchange for all the goods he has brought to market, while it is none the less what he needs that he consults in the first instance, with respect to the goods he intends to acquire in the course of his transactions. — Menger

II. Attempts at Solution Hitherto

We could say money as a commodity arose due to a state-sanctioned law and that it was born out of regard for the common interest. But this conflicts with the fact that:

  1. Common interest is a long-term game, whereas an individual’s purchases are usually to meet short-term needs and wants
  2. So far there hasn’t been any historical record of sweeping laws stating the selection of precious metals, why they were selected, and how they were adapted for monetary purposes

So how did precious metals, relative to every other commodity, become general knowledge as mediums of exchange and currency?

III. The problem of the genesis of a medium of exchange

If you happened to be ‘an economic man’:

  • What can be bargained is limited by what you directly need (i.e. not what you don’t need, or what you already have in good supply)
  • Rarely would you meet another person who’s selling what you need, and who also happens to want exactly what you’re selling
    Even under a system of bartering, the exchange would loop: A wants something owned by B, who needs another thing owned by C, but C wants what A has…

The above issues would’ve stopped commerce and the production of unpopular goods. But they didn’t. This is because the theory of money underlying the supposed issues assume that goods are without differences and are equally and absolutely saleable. What’s proposed is that commodities in general take on different degrees of saleableness (Absatzfahigkeit), which, more broadly, reflects why money has an almost unlimited saleableness.

IV. Commodities as more or less saleable

The ease of selling at a market at any convenient time at current purchasing prices with more or less loss in purchasing price = "more or less saleable".

We can’t buy and sell commodities at will, for any price, at any time in a given market, even for marketable items. The purchasing price is usually never equivalent to the selling price, as the latter will always be at a loss (hence why the buyer has greater power over the seller). The selling itself is dictated by the general economic price of the market, as well as the quantity and demand of your goods (it’s much easier to sell corn than Sanskrit writings). Time is also an important factor - if you can wait it out, you’ll eventually be able to sell any wares at economic price regardless of current demand or saleableness. Other factors which also keep the saleableness of commodities elastic include market development, speculation, nicheness etc.

V. Concerning the Causes of the different degrees of saleableness in commodities

Why some commodities can be sold more easily in some markets and others can’t?

The 6 circumstances in which a commodity can command a sale at any time:

  1. How many people want it; the extent, intensity and recurrence of their desire

  2. Purchasing power of the above individuals

  3. The supply vs demand

  4. Divisibility of commodity and its capacity to be tailored to individual customer needs

  5. Market development and speculation

  6. Number and nature of political and social limitations imposed on the commodity's exchange and consumption

Spatial constraints of saleableness:

  1. The degree to which the want is disturbed in space

  2. The degree of transportability and the costs of transport relative to value of goods

  3. How developed transport and commerce are regarding different classes of commodities

  4. Degree of development and inter-communication of organised (local) markets through "arbitrage" (purchasing a good in one place, then selling it at another where it is worth more)

  5. Degree of restrictions on inter-communication of different goods in commercial interlocal and international trade

Temporal constraints of saleableness:

  1. How enduring is the customers’ need (regardless of fluctuation of need)

  2. Durability of the commodity itself

  3. Cost of durability and storing

  4. Rate of interest

  5. Periodicity of a market for the rate of interest

  6. Speculation development, especially related time-bargains

  7. Political and social restrictions on commodity movement across time periods

VI. On the Genesis of Media of Exchange

Humans are driven by their individual economic interests rather than thoughts about the common good or state intervention. Relatively scarce commodities are usually in greater, more constant and effective demand. Therefore, it’s in the interest of those willing and able to trade to own these types of commodities to better obtain the goods they want with greater ease and certainty. This is done either through 1) direct exchange, or 2) oblique exchange (first obtaining mediate goods of higher saleableness than what they currently own to allow more flexibility). Since trade is increasingly extended in space and over longer intervals of time, bartering less saleable goods for those that have a wide range of saleableness in time and place seems a robust strategy. Not only would you be safe in the present market, but owning goods with distributed demand provides yourself future options over all other market-goods at economic prices. Therefore it seems that the saleableness of one commodity is only due to the relatively inferior saleableness of every other commodity.

Future-proofing by way of exchanging less saleable commodities for those more saleable makes sense. It’s in the interest of everyone to accept the medium of exchange, and everyone actually does. But, this also assumes that individuals who willingly accept the commodity acting as the medium of exchange (which is relatively useless to them):

  1. is already aware of their economic interest
  2. have the ability to not only recognise why some commodities are advantageous but also why and which commodities relative to others are the best mediums of exchange i.e. which commodities will bring them closer to what they need

Therefore, how some commodities win out as mediums are when economically-discerning individuals recognise commodities with particular attributes as viable (versus all other saleable goods), and use them to achieve their interests through the right methods over a period of time. Others catch on and recognise these as advantageous, and so the commodity will experience a virtuous circle of increasing acceptance in the community. Thus, it can be said that practice, tradition and habit are the drivers of change and instilled the intention of exchange. Across history and culture, goods which became acceptable and accepted media of exchange ranged from the substance used, the shape, to certain kinds of coins.

People find out their own economic interests through others — Menger

VII. The process of differentiation between commodities which have become media of exchange and the rest

Commodities that pull ahead in the saleableness competition become “money”, but once they do, their original saleableness increases substantially. Now, not only do your less saleable wares in the marketplace have value if they’re easily converted into money, but you’re better positioned to purchase other goods (which you certainly can do since money has high saleableness). The goods themselves are determined by the general economic price, which you’re unable to control. The literal and metaphorical costs of coming to market without money increases - your two options are to 1) pay the penalty upfront or 2) play the long game until you can convert.

Being able to buy what we want and whenever we want is a “habit of economic action”, a false assuredness that makes us believe we have control over commodities. But we’re mostly unconscious of our compulsory purchases while compulsory sales force themselves into consciousness. In reality, commodities are subject to time and price and so are relatively, if not absolutely, inherently uncertain.

Again, commodities that are saleable enough to become money rely on the relative saleableness to other goods. This differentiation is significant and further emphasised by the state once money is in circulation.

VIII. How the precious metals became money

Besides being ornamental, precious metals have a lot going for them in terms of saleableness and thus became popular mediums of exchange:

  • natural scarcity (thus demand is always relatively high)
  • well-distributed geographically
  • ease of extraction and elaboration versus other metals

The individuals who could buy were also those who could barter more effectively and so created a greater desire for precious metals, expanding the saleable distribution over time and space (of course individuals with a lower bartering ability could also proportionately constrain demand).

Not only were precious metals popular in the marketplace, but other features such as their colour, weight and transportability contributed to their introduction into state-managed economy once enough supply had been collected. The capacity to take a durable stamp for quality control also raised their saleableness and eased diffusion, distribution and adoption as money in economically advanced nations.

IX. Influence of the sovereign power

Precious metals have primarily become adapted into money through social institution. However, it’s only through state recognition and regulation has the function of money been perfected. The fractional adjustments to the infinite, variable needs of an evolving system has only been possible through laws enabling the fixing of coinage to include all grades of value, establishing coined pieces to win public confidence, mitigating risk of fraud and, most importantly, maintaining circulation.

Bio: Carl Menger (1840-1921) — Austrian economist, founded the Austrian School of economics which opposed theories of classical economists like Adam Smith. His contributions to the discipline were developing theories of marginal utility and the subjective theory of value. He also laid the groundwork for following economists like Mises, Hayek, and Rothbard.
