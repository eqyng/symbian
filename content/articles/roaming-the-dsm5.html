Roaming the DSM-5

Proposing a knowledge management system for the DSM

Last Tended: 08.08.21

Project DSM to Roam

*What:* Proposing a more transparent system for the DSM

*Why:* The diagnostic process can be updated for the contemporary clinician

*How:* Applying the DSM to knowledge-work software

The DSM-5

The Diagnostics and Statistical Manual (DSM), alongside the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), are the clinical handbooks for diagnosing mental health conditions and disorders. Both are updated every several years to reflect developments in the field with current editions being the DSM-5 and ICD-10. These manuals are important as they create a common criteria and language when describing conditions - helpful for instances like cross-consultations between different clinicians. However, there have been contentions, such as the criteria being considered too categorical and/or arbitrary, the argument that diagnoses are subjective, and other proposals like the need to include indigenous perspectives in Western conceptions of mental health. My current observation, however, is that the DSM-5’s digital data architecture could be better structured to support a clinician’s diagnostic workflow.

Fig 1. As an aside, look at this - not sure if it’s just my computer but the ad banners, people…all the information is being shunted into a small window

The online DSM-5 mirrors the layout of a physical book - a table of contents as a ‘front page’, and a Sections menu within each disorder that details its subcategories. Ordinarily there would be no personal gripes if this was say, a maths textbook where knowledge is sequentially built upon. But the DSM-5 is a reference manual that is frequently accessed, and accessed in non-linear ways to diagnose patients. The current online version creates two problems for the contemporary clinician: 1) the manual is not set out the way a clinician would access material i.e. requiring specific information at specific times, and 2) technology is not being fully leveraged to show information on a higher level of granularity and in different representations. As is the case here, analogue processes and resources do not always map well when directly translated to the digital world.

Asides from the functional uses of the manual, a clinician has two main needs - to identify relational information between disorders, then use said information to make comparisons and inform their diagnosis. From my understanding, making a diagnosis requires:

  1. Contextual information via references from other clinicians, their own observations of the patient and interviews to better understand patient history
  2. Based on the provided information and most obvious cues, a range of educated guesses of several disorders can be made
  3. The patient’s stated or observed symptoms are systematically compared to the diagnostic criteria specified in the DSM. If the patient meets all of the criterion, and within the timeframes, the clinician makes a primary diagnosis of the most likely disorder
  4. If other specifiers are present, a secondary diagnosis can be suggested
  5. Treatment plans will then be recommended based on their empirical efficacy and the patient’s preferences

Steps 3 and 4 seem to be the most challenging to unravel. Mental health conditions can often co-occur with each other as they stem from similar patterns of vulnerabilities. The patient may therefore have multiple possible diagnoses as indicative responses and behaviours can be present across several disorders. Additionally, the current design layout and searchability of the online DSM-5 makes diagnosing and comparing comorbidities difficult or, at the very least, needlessly laborious. The clinician must check the individual criterion of many disorders, alongside their exclusion and inclusion guidelines, against the patient’s symptoms.

Fig 2. Comorbidities are set out in a list. The clinician would then need to go into each category to check the criteria and see if the patient matches each one. For some conditions there can be as many as 11 criterion

The worst case scenario that this complexity presents is a misdiagnosis on the basis of incomplete information. Even if the patient is rediagnosed, it means they are spending longer in distress without proper treatment. A workaround might be to open multiple tabs or hold the comparisons in working memory to make the evaluation, but this is inefficient and may lead to the aforementioned symptom slippage. Therefore, there needs to be alternative ways of representing DSM-5 data that is both flexible enough to accommodate unpredictable methods of access as well as stable enough for comparisons between conditions.

Application to software

Digital information is often stashed away in nested folders on desktops - these storage habits are relics of the analogue world. The lack of visibility and general accessibility means there is a high probability that the information may be forgotten, or not easily retrieved when needed most. My concern is the risk of patients falling through the cracks of comorbidities. However, it may be mitigated with a more streamlined way of 1) retrieving and 2) comparing information. For the reorganisation, these two tasks of clinical workflow were targeted as they present the highest risks.

*Attempt I: Excel —* Excel was first used in an effort to fulfil the comparative aspect within and between disorders. For instance, there are three subtypes within Bipolar Disorder - Bipolar I, Bipolar II and Cyclothymic Disorder - with nuances in the duration and presence of manic and hypomanic episodes. The labelled sheets are the different categories for quick comparisons between Bipolar Disorder and related disorders such as Depression. However, Excel does not fulfil the access aspect - the amount of data that would need to be embedded within one cell is not feasible and, as mentioned previously, there can be 11 or more diagnostic criterion. Furthermore, the ability to search for related disorders is limited to manual searches. Excel was therefore deemed unsuccessful.

Fig 3. A clinician would probably be better off using the online DSM-5 with the amount of scrolling they’d need to do on this Excel sheet if this was actually completed

*Attempt II: Roam Research — Roam Research (Roam) seems to be the best attempt so far. In brief, Roam is a relatively new knowledge-working software (a “note-taking tool”), and approaches digital information storage as “networked thought” rather than archives. Each note is visible as a flattened hierarchy and are linked together every time a key idea is referenced in other notes. This is like someone laying out all the notes they have ever collected on a large table. Similarly to the brain’s neural nodes, the network facilitates emergent patterns, drawing together topics that may have previously seemed disparate and representing them as clusters. Instead of potentially hoarding outdated information in folders, the aim is to make data more live and fluid (more in-depth explanations of Roam). On an individual level, people are using Roam as a means to organise digital information, metacognition and for sparking creativity as novel connections are made between ideas. When applied to the DSM-5 for occupational purposes, the networked structure enables efficient access, while the transparency may assist in unravelling comorbidities.

Fig 4, networked thought

A few changes were necessary due to the lack of contents page. The broad diagnosis is in capital letters to differentiate it from the conditions in lowercase letters. The emojis are tags used to additionally group the conditions together in a visual manner. For instance, Depressive Disorders is the overall diagnostic domain, with Major Depressive Disorder and Persistent Depressive Disorder as the types of depression. In the case of depressive disorders, a purple umbrella emoji was used as the visual tag.

1. Efficient access to relational information:

As before, a clinician identifies several possible disorders and must propose a primary diagnosis given the available information. The table of contents of the DSM-5’s current presentation only categorises different conditions within their respective domains — for example, Trauma- and Stressor-related Disorders would only show 2 other disorders - Acute Stress and Posttraumatic Stress Disorder - within its chapter. This effectively silos the disorders and makes comparisons across categories less efficient as a clinician would need to first go into the broad domain, then into the disorder of interest. A network view presents all of the disorders in one view, allowing patterns to surface between conditions more easily. For example, Roam’s Graph Overview clearly shows that Posttraumatic Stress Disorder is related to 15 other conditions. The clinician can therefore easily access either the related broad domain (Trauma- & Stressor-related Disorders, Depressive Disorders, Anxiety Disorders, Bipolar & Related Disorders or Personality Disorders) or the specific conditions as needed.

Fig 5. Clicking on a node shows the other nodes it is directly related to due to the inclusion of key terms

2. Granularity of context:

As in Fig 2., the comorbidities are currently set out in a list within individual disorders. Although the information is stated, it does not show the complete crosslinks between the disorders for both comorbidities and differential diagnoses. As previously alluded to, this would necessitate the use of multiple tabs or the clinician’s memory to draw the relations. The bidirectional feature of Roam is useful in this instance because not does it show where the target condition was mentioned in other pages, but also provides the surrounding context that it was drawn from. For example, within a Roam note of Conduct Disorder, ADHD appears as comorbid while Separation Anxiety Disorder is a differential diagnosis.

Fig 6. Comorbidities for Conduct Disorder

Fig 7. Differential diagnoses for Conduct Disorder

The granularity of context thus clarifies what the similarities between Conduct Disorder and ADHD are as well as how Separation Anxiety Disorder is different on the one page. In this way, a clinician is better able to decide whether a patient’s Conduct Disorder symptoms lean more towards an ADHD or towards a Separation Anxiety Disorder diagnosis without having to flick back and forth between the all the conditions.

*Discussion:*

The primary use case was for an easier and more efficient diagnostic process of mental health conditions. Using a network to visualise connections across conditions enables related information to be better captured. Additionally, the interactive graph and distinguishable nodes allows clinicians to access different conditions as needed. However, there was also an unexpected finding from creating the graph:

Fig 8. I did my best to make the emojis relate to the disorder but I wasn’t sacrificing visibility for relevancy. The categories just needed to stand out from each other

The Graph Overview shows a visual representation of the DSM-5 information. Each node is a note, and the size of the node indicates how many times the note’s title has been referenced as a key term in other notes. Judging by the size of each node, the conditions through which all roads lead through seem to mainly be:

  1. Substance use disorders
  2. Anxiety disorder
  3. Major depressive disorder
    There are several other conditions of significance such as Posttraumatic stress disorder, Bipolar & related disorders, Depressive disorders, Personality disorders, and Obsessive-Compulsive disorder

With this interpretation, there is the potential for targeted treatment of disorders that cause the most knock-on effects to other conditions. For instance, more funding may be funneled towards the treatment of Substance use Disorder, Anxiety disorder, or Major Depressive Disorder. Alternatively, future research questions can be generated from the Graph Overview, exploring the intersections of different conditions such as relations between panic attacks and bipolar disorders.

Considerations:

There are a few caveats to take into account. The current Roam graph is incomplete as it only includes the DSM-5 information that I needed for my university exams. It also does not include other manuals that clinicians would use to cross-reference such as the ICD. Furthermore, the categories may change in future editions of the DSM and ICD - for instance, the diagnosis structure used to diagnose schizophrenia disorders may be changed from a categorical representation to a spectrum in the ICD-11. If this is the case, it may be difficult to represent spectrums within a Roam graph since the tool uses an inherently categorical model of weighted nodes. The largest critique of this solution is that it operates under the assumption clinicians find diagnosing comorbidities difficult (I personally found it difficult to parse out the murky differences for my university assignments). This may be true for those beginning their career, but it may not be so for experienced professionals. In future, other note-taking tools should be used to see if more alternative depictions of DSM data can be created.

DSM-5 Roam Graph v2:

As it stands, the DSM can still be improved by including different and more flexible views of data. For the second iteration, all entries from the DSM-5 must be included. Additionally, the differences between conditions as a broad diagnosis, conditions as a disorder as well as distinguishing visual tags (e.g. Anxiety Disorder is referenced in other conditions as a general umbrella term, but there are also several conditions within it such as Generalised Anxiety and Social Anxiety) need to be more concretely defined.

All in all, this was a fun side project that started when I was studying for my Abnormal Psychology exams.
