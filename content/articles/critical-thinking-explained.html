Critical Thinking Explained

on Inductive, Deductive & Abductive Reasoning

 06 Sep '25

I’d heard the term “critical thinking” being bandied around for a while now (mainly in regards to education, how critical thinking skills must be taught (better) and that ChatGPT may be shrinking our grey matter… a topic for another day).

But quite frankly, I didn’t really understand what the term actually meant.

A Brief History

In 1910, John Dewey, an educational reformer and psychologist, championed the idea of critical thinking as an objective for American schooling. He regarded ‘reflective thinking’ as a necessary skill for (young) people to build meaningful lives, and become effective, purposeful members of society.

Critical thinking is a toolkit, a discipline and an attitude. Students were encouraged to solve problems with intellectual honesty, develop testable hypotheses and constantly scrutinise their beliefs in light of new evidence. It was more about developing the self-initiative to articulate problems, consider alternative solutions and guide thoughtful action based on informed decisions, rather than merely learning lessons.

“Much of our thinking, left to itself, is biased…uninformed or down-right prejudiced. Yet the quality of our life and that of which we produce…depends precisely on the quality of our thought.”
Paul & Elder (2007)

Critical Thinking in Practice

From the 1930s to 60s, several taxonomies were created by American college examiners to define and categorise cognitive skills as well as standardise school test criteria. As an example, the first was Bloom’s Taxonomy, a dynamic 6-level framework aimed at moving students from knowledge gatherers (lower-order thinking) to intentional evaluators (higher-order thinking). The final goal was for the student to become a creative contributor of goods or ideas in service of society.

University of Conneticut

Creative thinking as a toolkit encompasses several skills such as data interpretation, analysis, synthesis, evaluation and decision-making. This is done through different styles of reasoning.

Three Reasoning Types

Inductive, deductive and abductive reasoning differ in the type of logic, process, and strength of the conclusion.

1. Inductive Reasoning

Inductive reasoning is rooted in the real world. When an event repeats, each case becomes a datapoint to help us recognise a pattern. When observed over time, the trend can be used to develop a theory. e.g. "I’ve seen multiple white swans, therefore swans are probably white".

Similarly, when in uncertain conditions, previous experience is generally all we have to work with. So we use history to project the future, allowing us to flexibly handle new information as it arises and observe any emergent patterns or behaviours.

However, conclusions can only ever tend towards certainty. More observations mean a stronger conclusion, but it’s never considered absolutely true. The theory can break with a single contrary observation e.g. A black swan. There may also be the unconscious tendency to cherry-pick examples that support our chosen conclusion, leading us further away from the truth.

To shore up this weakness, we need to be open to revising our theories with new evidence, and ensure we’ve gathered true examples from a large sample size of data.

2. Deductive Reasoning

Every physicist, consultant, mathematician and lawyer knows this style well. Rather than the ‘bottom-up’ approach, deductive reasoning works in the opposite manner, relying on a finite set of premises that lead logically towards a conclusion. These principles act as a ‘blueprint’, so when applied to different or new scenarios, the user can problem-solve for an answer every time.

The structure of logic is a “syllogism”:

  • Premise 1 (P1): An assumption about the world
  • Premise 2 (P2): An assumption about the world with a term in common with P1
  • Conclusion: drawn from the above assumptions

An example would be, P1: All dogs are mammals, P2: All mammals are animals, Conclusion: Therefore all dogs are animals. This is a valid example as the premises are true and the logic is sound.

When the structure is followed, the listener can follow along with clarity, so the solution is presented as a logical, strong and persuasive.

However, deductive reasoning can restrict thinking. Given the “top-down” approach, the premises confine the types of solutions explored and information surfaced. If surprise evidence appears, it may slip through the cracks or be ‘explained away’ as it does not fit into the predefined conceptual buckets. False premises lend themselves to misleading conclusions, despite how reasonable the logic seems. For instance, P1: All dogs are animals; P2: All animals have four legs; C: Therefore all dogs have four legs.

Regardless of how confidently a conclusion is asserted, we need to be sure we agree on the initial premises and question their assumptions e.g. asking your conversational partner how the conclusion would differ if the premises were changed.

3. Abductive Reasoning

Most of our daily experience means making decisions on incomplete information. So when we happen across an unexpected observation, we often create several hypotheses to explain why. Since reasons can’t all be true at once, we choose the explanation that best fits the current evidence as a working theory that’s open to revision.

For instance, if a friend is late to our meeting, we might think there is traffic, maybe they had gotten ill or they just woke up late. Each reason is equally plausible. But until our friend confirms with us and provides more information, we make an educated guess: “Knowing them, they like to sleep in. So they’re probably still sleeping and that’s why they’re late.” Despite there being alternative answers, we were able to infer which made the most sense given what we know of our friend. Stanford d.school | Tessa Forshaw

The Differences

Reasoning is a lever for progress, and never used in isolation. We employ multiple at any time as complementary tools since they all have their strengths and weaknesses. Whether we’re using it to make patterns, apply principles or making a best guess, they are tools for clear decision-making, discernment and value-aligned action.
