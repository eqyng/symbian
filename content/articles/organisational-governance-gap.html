The Organisational Governance Gap

AI compliance needs a new model of organisational memory

 31 Aug '26

In this AI era, governance frameworks are failing in a unique manner.

There is a structural mismatch between the way organisations have historically documented knowledge or project decisions, and governance's core question of "Are your decisions justified?"

As someone who's recently worked through governance requirements for an AI feature in a regulated industry, I've experienced this mismatch. Governance required that I answer things like noting the guardrails, access controls and tests for drift monitoring.

As part of the team who inherited, but now owned the feature, I ran into three issues. I didn't have full context for certain design decisions made by the initial team well over a year ago. Not only that, but the introduction of assessments was recent, so the required information wasn't captured in a way our governance team needed, and I needed to reconstruct the decision-making. Further, it was an effortful and time-expensive discovery process. I needed to trawl through different links trying to find relevant artefacts, distinguishing between whether the documents actually existed or not or whether I had access issues. If it wasn't a permissions problem, there were times where it was a very human problem - the only people who could answer my questions about decisions had completely forgotten the context.

Core Issues

Modern enterprises are structured differently in that 'organisational memory' becomes an issue:

1. Information Fragmentation

Information lived in different places - documents, powerpoint slides, email threads, Confluences pages, excel sheets, Zoom meetings, memories. These were sometimes gated, lost or otherwise inaccessible.

2. Informal Decision-making

A secondary issue related to governance is decision traceability, which answers why certain actions were taken over others, rationale, assumptions and alternatives considered. Important rationale and discussions lived in discussions where the final decision may have been documented, but the reasoning behind them weren't.

3. Context decay

Over the passage of time, usual business processes like staff turnover, role changes, leave etc leads to an an inherent knowledge asymmetry between new and old team members. During the project, documentation may be written in such a way that is legible and understandable to the author and their team members. However, for other staff who may read it later, the assumptions are no longer obvious. These decisions now require reconstruction and interpretation which may or may not have been how the original authors intended it.

The Problem of Organisational Knowledge

Knowledge creation doesn't necessarily mean adequate knowledge capture, transfer or discovery.

Company knowledge bases are usually stored as wiki-style pages (Sharepoint and Confluence pages) where information can be written and consolidated. Decision Logs, Architectural Decision Records (ADRs) and Lessons Learned documents are attempts to track decision owners, approvals, decisions, improvements and successes.

However, even if information is centralised, over time wiki-like pages become sprawling beasts of links which may not be easily accessible, discovered, regularly updated, referred to later or organised well. Documentation becomes a burden for projects moving at speed, with shifting priorities and under deadline pressures.

While organisations are generally good at preserving artefacts, they are poor at preserving the reasoning that connects them. This is costly for future teams who only have outcomes to reconstruct more amorphous details like what lessons were learned, what information can still be trusted.

Most governance frameworks are designed around these artefacts being the only measurable, traceable and auditable chain of reasoning. The unfortunate assumption is not that there's enough documentation, but the correct kind of documentation available to make an informed justification in the first place.

Knowledge Management Models

The problem of organisational knowledge is an old one.

Knowledge Management Models (KMMs) are processes that aim to retain implicit (tacit) and explicit knowledge as valuable levers of competitiveness, productivity and effectiveness (Haslinda & Sarinah, 2009). Explicit knowledge is clearly codified whereas tacit knowledge manifests as assumptions and expectations that underlie an organisation's structure.

Existing models such as Boisot's Knowledge Category Models (1987), Nonaka's Knowledge Management Model (1995) and Demerest's Knowledge Management Model (1999) attempt to articulate the flow of knowledge in organisations and society. The models vary in representations from 2x4 matrices spanning tacit and explicit knowledge, as well as across individual and organisational domains to flowcharts.

However, there is a temporal aspect that's missing from these models, where the 'knowledge spiral' of knowledge dissemination is assumed to be unidirectional from individual to organisation, and remains consistently clear across time.

The current issue that AI governance has surfaced is that information that was once explicit can become increasingly uninterpretable unless intentionally documented. And that implicit knowledge can be easily derived from explicit artefacts. But rarely is it someone's deliverable to document the reasoning steps that led up to a decision.

We need a new method of enquiry. KMS map whether organisations have existing knowledge management practices and inputs as documentation. But they don't measure whether there is enough context for governance questions to be answered. An Organisational Memory Maturity Model (OMMM) shifts the focus from process maturity to epistemic maturity, defined by the sophistication of questions the organisation can answer with what artefacts remain.

  1. Knowledge storage - How can I save information? Information is in Sharepoint, documents, teams chats, notebooks, people's heads which disappears when they leave.

  2. Knowledge retrieval - Where is the information? Co-pilot can synthesise information from various parts of the business.

  3. Knowledge reconstruction - What is the decision? What evidence is needed to reconstruct the context and defend reasoning without needing to read through several different documents?

  4. Wisdom - What are the learnings from past projects? How can information be integrated and discoverable across the entire organisation over time? These learnings can be applied and transferred in different contexts but for the same underlying problem.

Where Governance Frameworks Suffer

Inadequate knowledge transfer creates tension between documented outcomes (Level 1 of OMMM: "What were the decisions made") and the goals of good governance (Level 4 of OMMM: "Was this decision justified?").

An accurate judgement of decision quality requires not just the decision but the surrounding context of all the tradeoffs, assumptions, comparisons and reasoning that led up to it.

Therefore, when an organisation only has explicit, fragmented documentation to reconstruct decisions from, governance is actually evaluating an unreliable proxy with deteriorating context. The ability to explain, defend and review these decisions degrades with it. A decision cannot be judged in a vacuum.

This broken feedback loop is where responsible AI and governance breaks down.

A Proposed Solution

From what I've observed, the current solution to the broken feedback loop is to document every detail to the minute degree i.e. knowledge storage.

Concrete example - I'd developed an evaluation rubric for the project I was on. A sample of 200 customer records would be gathered and assessed across 5 broad categories, and 18 sub-categories. Each record will be manually marked as a Success or Failure. Successes would be recorded at a category level, with the implication that all sub-categories were fulfilled, and key information included. A general comment as to why it's marked a Success would be written. Failures are classed as at least one sub-category failing, which would be recorded, the criterion(s) identified and the verbatim transcript noted. This was an exception-based approach, where more effort would be funnelled into clarifying deviations, rather than the norm.

Our Risk function stated we must be recording sub-category information for both Successes and Failures i.e. noting every detail. I considered this enormous effort for a small team, given the criteria was already quite specific. For 200 records this would be 3600 lines of documentation overhead.

The question then becomes "How do we preserve enough context such that the decision can be reconstructed later as faithfully as possible" i.e. how do we preserve minimum sufficient decision memory?

One way to address the epistemic maturity problem is by accounting for different types of teams and their particular position in time. A tool I've created is a structured, prompt-based Co-pilot notebook that uses AI as an automated retroactive assistant. The goal is to ensure that the context and learnings remain discoverable regardless of when the project is picked up.

How this differs from a standard Confluence page is that it actively synthesises and reconstructs implicit reasoning from existing but disparate explicit artefacts at multiple points in time as context disintegrates. It also distinguishes between what can and can't be reliably reconstructed. Therefore, rather than writing everything down, the ability to quickly understand project details and context in future is optimised.

The process:

  1. Initiating team creates a new Notebook that includes my prompt and all project-related documents as references. The prompt steps through specific sections like the business context, problem, project timelines, decisions, risks, and outcomes. Documents can include informal information channels such as Zoom meetings, specific chats, email threads etc.
  2. Enter a prompt like "Generate report".
  3. Receive an easily scannable AI-generated report that provides a clear overview.

The Notebook benefits 3 types of audiences - Teams who initiate the project, teams who inherit the project at a later date, and future teams at an unknown point in the future.

The value proposition and questions answered for each audience varies.

  • Initiating teams can reflect on the project in terms of what went well or what can be improved.
  • Inheriting teams have a better starting point for triaging knowledge, and can understand what information exists, what is missing, what happened and why.
  • Future teams now have a library of patterns and approaches to search from to see whether a problem they're facing has a similar shape. Trends of risks that repeatedly occur can be identified, and knowledge can transfer across domains.
