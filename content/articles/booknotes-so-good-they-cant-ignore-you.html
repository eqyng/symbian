Book Notes - So Good They Can't... (Newport, 2012)

Newport's 4 rules to building a great career

 05 Oct '21

Last Tended: 16.10.21

Summary of the Summary:

Mastery borne from deliberate practice will trump passion in the occupational arena. As you build rare and valuable skills you can use the successes and failures from projects to course correct. Think of this exploratory phase as placing educated bets on different missions that are viable for you personally. Once you’ve gained enough expertise you’ll be able to recognise where you’re best suited given your skillset i.e. work backwards, not towards a mission. If you can adopt a systematic approach to becoming better, you’ll get so good they can’t ignore you.

…working right trumps finding the right work…[find] a better approach to the work already available

Summary Notes:

RULE #1: PASSION =/= PROGRESS

Passion is more a side effect of mastery

Steve Job’s Passion Hypothesis of “do what you love and the money will follow” didn’t mention that both Wozniak and Jobs kept their regular 9-5 while working on Apple on the side. They hadn’t expected the ‘small-time’, ‘low-risk’ venture to take off.

From the Self Determination Theory, Daniel Pink (2011). The more you become competent at your work, the more autonomy is granted and the more connected you feel to your peers. This becomes a virtuous circle of creating work that’s a vital part of your life and identity.

“Things happen in stages...your problem is that you're trying to judge all things in the abstract before you do them. That's your tragic mistake" - Ira Glass

RULE #2: BECOME A CRAFTSMAN

Build Career Capital

Focus on skills so you can offer the world high-quality output. Stay away from professional roles that:

a. Do not offer you skill development opportunities
b. Focuses on something you think is useless or actively bad for the world
c. Forces you to work with people you really dislike

Work in the centre of your industry

it’s the best place to learn what it’ll take to succeed and maximise career capital accrual. But you also don’t need elaborate career plans. Carefully expand your skillset, look for whoever is interested in you, then pursue which opportunity seems most promising.

Deliberate practice

Is the key skill to building career capital. It involves an “activity designed, typically by a teacher, for the sole purpose of effectively improving specific aspects of an individual's performance“ (Ericsson, 2016; Gladwell, 2008). Other ways to supplement the training philosophy of deliberate practice is by soliciting feedback, taking on multiple projects, and being strategic about your time to ensure you focus on what’s important (not simply “reacting to what’s immediate”).

Follow the 5 Habits of a Craftsman:

  1. Identify the market you’re competing in. Are you in a capital (competitive but winner takes all) or auction (different types of career capital in which someone can create a unique collection) market? This way you can figure out how to expend your energy to succeed
  2. Seek already open avenues to build skills faster
  3. What’s your definition of “good“? Use that as goalposts
  4. Get comfortable with the uncomfortable (deliberate practice, constant feedback and competition)
  5. Be patient. Career capital acquisition is the long game

Diligence is less about paying attention to your main pursuit, and more about your willingness to ignore other pursuits that pop up along the way to distract you. — Martin

RULE #3: FIGHT FOR EMPLOYMENT AUTONOMY

The Importance of Control

Get your head in the ROWE (Results-Only-Work-Environment). Control is a trait that defines good work, and helps you gain autonomy over what you do and how you do it.

Don’t pursue control before building career capital

Unconventional lifestyles are appealing because they appear to give you a lot of autonomy. But there’s actually a lot of complexity behind it. You need exchangeable skills so this bold career decision is sustainable. Enthusiasm is not enough, the details don’t just work themselves out. Your bids for autonomy will generate resistance If your (previous) employer is resisting your bids then you’ve most likely become valuable enough to gain meaningful control over your working life.

When no one cares what you do with your working life you probably don't have enough career capital to do anything interesting.

The Law of Financial Viability — the amount of control you have is proportionate to how many people will pay you. Money is “a neutral indicator of value” (Derek Sivers) and how valuable your skills are.

RULE 4: THINK SMALL, ACT BIG

Make your life a mission

Simply generating career capital is not enough, you also need to create a mission by making careful bets and keeping a marketing mindset. Experiment with protomissions to find a direction worth pursuing for you. It’ll help provide a unifying focus, see you through the strain of hard work and provide meaning beyond the lab. Because missions are hard. So take the time to plan for it and confidently take action.

Missions Require Capital

Start with the “small” by focusing on a selection of subjects (for a potentially long time). Then once you get to the cutting edge of your field and start seeing innovations you can pursue a suitable mission with “big” action. The disciplinary frontier, or the ‘adjacent possible’ (Johnson, 2010), is almost always discovered by multiple people at the edge as they reformulate old ideas into new ways. You can be one of these experts too.

Missions Require "Little Bets"

(Sims, 2011) People often already have some measure of career capital, meaning there could be a number of missions available to them. But they may not have a strategy to explore all these avenues to build a solid career. Small projects help you concretely explore the possibilities of a new idea and see which ones have the highest chances of success. The success or failure is important feedback on which to pursue or abandon.

Missions Require Marketing

To create a successful mission portfolio tailored to you, fill it with projects that satisfy the marketing Law of Remarkability i.e. it’s worth noticing and people can talk about it in a venue where remarking is easy, like Twitter or at conferences. What’s your purple cow? (Seth Godin, 2005), why do some ideas in your field fail and others don’t?

bored, available and ambitious - a dangerous combination. The traits that can make your life interesting have very little to do with intensive soul-searching

Conclusion

In the spirit of Feynman, Newport created his own career capital strategy to hone his craft. By working to understand theories from the bottom up, he could understand whole swathes of work. Newport’s deliberate practice strategies include:

  1. Do top level research to understand the edges and potential of new theories
  2. Engage in exploratory projects that can be completed in less than a month. By the end not only will you have created a product of value and concrete proof towards skill mastery, but the outcomes will also provide feedback on next steps. Keep 2-3 projects active at a time for intense attention and create deadlines to instill a sense of urgency. Newport spent 15 hours of deliberate practice (each week, presumably) and tallied the total hours he spent each month on projects so he could improve on the numbers the next month
  3. Expose yourself to new ideas in the field by brainstorming (Newport took bets on new theory results), read papers, attend talks. Newport mapped the relatedness between different mathematical proofs, created quizzes until he could memorise key definitions, and added weekly detailed summarisations of papers in his own words to his Research Bible

As a knowledge worker you must be more intentional about incorporating deliberate practice into your working life. Instead of being wholly productivity-centric, where you’re doing things but not getting better at your work, turn your focus to becoming craft-centric.

My Thoughts:

Newport’s book could be summarised into 1) build skills until mastery and 2) complete protomissions to figure out where you’ll be best suited (after a while the chapters feel a bit same same, maybe this is why self-help books are seen as a bit…hm).

One part of me thinks the argument sounds reminiscent of a hustle-at-all-costs culture. Another part agrees that “passion” is a misleading word because it’s vague and ill-defined. Hence the need for experimental pathways to narrow down your options. I’m assuming what makes a skill rare and valuable is how difficult it is to obtain or learn (with money as an ‘objective’ measure).

At the same time I think the issue of balancing the tradeoff between time and effort still stands. It’s difficult to know whether skills that are rare and valued today will be rare and valued tomorrow or in ten years. There are skills that are transferable of course, but would that necessitate extra time learning how to transition? And how might you define what’s “good” for other fields like history or social science? Ericsson’s theory of deliberate practice might seem suited for professional sports or even mathematics, but doesn’t seem to be quite as aligned for humanities. The most Newport says is that if knowledge workers figure out a strategy they’ll blow past their peers. But I suppose the number of careers is too extensive for even brief pointers for all of them.

There were a few mixed reviews on Goodreads. Personally, I quite enjoy reading books like this. It reminds me that there are plenty of people out there working hard to make things happen, and this is one strategy.
